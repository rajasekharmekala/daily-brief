### Jensen Huang Steps Down After Thirty-Three Years, and Nvidia Faces Its First Post-Founder Test

Let us begin with what Nvidia has confirmed. On Thursday, November 12, the company announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive officer, and board member, effective immediately. He co-founded Nvidia in 1993. That is thirty-three years at the helm, ended with one announcement and no visible transition period.

Longtime Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress is now interim chief executive while the board searches for a permanent successor. No timetable for that search has been made public. This is not an ordinary succession. Nvidia supplies the chips beneath much of the frontier AI industry, so who runs it is a question for every customer, supplier, and investor in the chain.

The market has already voted in its own way. Nvidia shares closed Friday, November 13, at about one hundred sixty-three dollars, down two percent on the day. That leaves the stock about a third below its 52-week high. The Nasdaq-100 fell more than one percent in the same session, so the shock did not stay inside one ticker.

Now, four key developments to hold in your head. Number one: the exit was immediate and total. Huang did not keep a board seat or an advisory title, at least as far as the company's announcement says. Companies that expect a smooth handoff usually announce a smooth handoff. This announcement read like the opposite.

Second, the reporting on why. Outlets including AppleInsider reported that the board had been scrutinizing Huang's personal relationships and potential security concerns ahead of a planned board vote. Nvidia has not laid out that case in public detail. We treat those accounts as reported, not as established fact, and we would urge you to do the same.

Third, the security angle widened on Sunday. According to the BBC, Megaspeed, Southeast Asia's largest buyer of Nvidia chips, said that Alice Huang, a suspected Chinese intelligence operative linked to the former chief executive, left her executive role at the company in August 2025. Megaspeed is stating a departure date, not answering every question around it. Which brings us to number four.

Number four is the policy exposure. Nvidia's most valuable products are also among the most tightly watched exports in the world. Any story that mixes the founder, a major chip buyer, and a suspected intelligence link will draw attention from regulators and lawmakers. We do not know yet whether any formal inquiry follows. We do know the story has now reached that neighborhood.

So how did Nvidia reach a moment like this? Start with key-person risk, the oldest problem in founder-led companies. For three decades, Huang was the strategy, the public face, and the final voice on product roadmaps. That kind of concentration is a strength while it works. It becomes a single point of failure when it does not, and boards rarely rehearse the failure case.

Interim leadership by a chief financial officer tells us something too. Kress knows the numbers, the customers, and the supply contracts as well as anyone alive. She is a steady choice for continuity. What an interim choice cannot do is set a decade-long technical direction, and Nvidia's customers are planning capital budgets that stretch well past the next two quarters.

Here is what we make of it. The business itself did not change overnight; the orders, the factories, and the software ecosystem are all still there. What changed is the discount investors apply to uncertainty. At a moment when ten-year Treasury yields are above five percent, that discount matters a great deal. We would watch three things: who the board names, whether customers publicly reaffirm their orders, and whether any regulator opens a formal inquiry. If all three break cleanly, this becomes a governance story. If any one turns ugly, it becomes an industry story. To see how Wall Street absorbed the shock on top of a bond market already under strain, we head to Desk II.

Nvidia's leadership shock did not hit Wall Street in a vacuum, and to see why, we turn now to Treasury yields above five percent and the stocks caught in that current in Desk II.

### CoreWeave's $41.6 Billion Recourse Debt and $846 Million Quarterly Interest Burden Expose Neocloud Math

CoreWeave's third-quarter 2026 release, published between November 10 and November 12, offered a headline that growth investors love. Revenue surged 168% year-over-year to $3.661 billion, and management raised its full-year outlook. On the surface, that looks like pure hypergrowth. Yet one line below the top-line celebration, the income statement told a far more sobering story.

GAAP operating income for the quarter came in at just $207 million. Over those exact same three months, net interest expense reached $846 million. Think about that ratio for a second. Interest costs ran more than four times operating profit. As a result, CoreWeave's net loss widened to $709 million, up sharply from $110 million a year earlier.

Demand is plainly real, yet the cost of financing that demand is eating the business alive. Look at the balance sheet to see why. Recourse debt jumped $10.2 billion in a single quarter, climbing from $31.4 billion on June 30 to $41.6 billion on September 30. Including $5.3 billion in non-recourse facilities, total debt now stands near $46.9 billion.

And the interest bill is still climbing. Management guided fourth-quarter interest expense to a range of $1.05 billion to $1.15 billion. That puts CoreWeave on a four-billion-dollar annual interest run rate before a single GPU is depreciated or replaced. Notice also that $41.6 billion of that borrowing sits as recourse corporate debt, not isolated project debt. Every lender can look straight through to the parent company if unit economics tighten.

Supporters point to customer deposits as the safety valve. CoreWeave already holds $10.0 billion in customer prepayments on its balance sheet. Management also expects roughly $9 billion more over the next three months. Those prepayments ease immediate liquidity pressure, yet they do not erase the underlying obligation. Instead, upfront cash converts financing risk into strict delivery risk.

### Google DeepMind Splits the Agent Stack: Gemini 4 Hydrogen, Dynamic Orchestrator, and the Single-Pass Decisions API

For the past two years, agent builders often forced a single frontier model to handle every step of a workflow. That meant paying heavy reasoning prices for high-level planning, routine file edits, and simple binary checks alike. On November 11 and November 12, Google DeepMind and the Antigravity team shipped a decisive break from that monolithic habit.

On November 12, Google DeepMind introduced Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a rapid-execution coding and agentic model. It is priced flat across a one-million-token context window at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens. At its default high thinking level, Hydrogen scores 51 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, edging past Gemini 4 Argon's 50. At its extra-high setting, it climbs to 54 while finishing tasks 2.5 times faster than Gemini 3.8 Flash.

Flat pricing across a full million tokens removes a common headache for agent harnesses. Developers no longer face sudden price cliffs when repository context or test logs expand mid-session. Hydrogen also keeps tool-call latency low enough that iterative edit-and-test loops feel interactive rather than batched.

Speed alone is only half the story. Alongside Hydrogen, Google Antigravity launched Orchestrator, a dynamic routing layer inside a single agent trajectory. Orchestrator switches bidirectionally between Gemini 4 Carbon for planning and recovery and Gemini 4 Hydrogen for code edits and test runs. On Terminal-Bench 4.0 and 4.1, that split achieves near-Carbon success rates while consuming 24% less quota and completing runs 19% faster.

That benchmark pairing matters for production teams. Terminal-Bench tests multi-step shell, repository, and debugging work where one wrong command can derail an entire session. Keeping Carbon success rates while shaving nearly a quarter off quota usage proves that speed and reliability do not require an all-or-nothing model choice.

Why does bidirectional switching matter so much? A static router guesses task difficulty once at the start and gets stuck with that choice. By contrast, a trajectory-level orchestrator can delegate ten routine tool calls to a fast executor, then escalate back to Carbon the instant a test suite throws an unexpected stack trace. Once Carbon repairs the plan, execution drops right back down to Hydrogen.

A day earlier, on November 11, DeepMind targeted an even bigger hidden tax in agent loops: structured evaluation and rubric checks. The new Gemini Decisions API, powered by gemini-decision-1, evaluates up to 1,000 closed-set yes-or-no, choice, or rubric questions across up to 256,000 multimodal tokens. Better yet, it does this in a single forward pass without token-by-token decoding.

Look at the economics of skipping autoregressive output generation. Gemini Decision 1 delivers 50 to 100 millisecond model compute latency, or 150 to 250 milliseconds end-to-end. It costs $0.10 per million input tokens with zero output tokens billed, making it nearly five times cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite with the Interactions API. Guardrails, content moderation, and multi-criteria evals suddenly run at cache-lookup speed.

For builders, the engineering lesson is immediate and practical. Stop paying frontier-planner prices and autoregressive latency for execution steps or closed-set classification. Separate your agent loop into three distinct tiers: heavy planning and recovery, fast tool execution, and single-pass rubric verification. Route every step to the cheapest tier that preserves end-to-end reliability.

### Agents Need a Flight Simulator: AWS Emulator and the Economics of Local Agent Sandboxes

When coding agents move from editing isolated functions to provisioning cloud infrastructure, the verification bottleneck moves with them. Running an untested script against a live cloud account risks real bills, leaked permissions, and slow feedback loops. On November 11, Amazon Web Services released AWS Emulator on GitHub to give agents a local flight simulator.

AWS Emulator provides an official local emulation layer for AWS APIs. Coding agents can test infrastructure and application changes locally without an active AWS account or live network calls. It ships with an installable agent skill requiring AWS CLI version 2.35.0 or higher, and engineers can run it as a local server or in embedded HTTP-client mode.

Why release an official emulator now? Because third-party mocks drift behind real cloud control planes, whereas agents need exact API semantics to catch IAM, state, and retry bugs before code ever leaves a laptop. When an agent can run twenty local dry runs in ten seconds, the cost of exploration drops near zero. Equally important, credentials never have to sit inside an autonomous loop just to run integration tests.

In a companion essay on Werner Vogels's All Things Distributed blog, AWS senior vice president Swaminathan Sivasubramanian framed what changes for human developers. As agents write a larger share of application code, the engineer's core job shifts toward designing faithful local verification environments and distributed-systems invariants. He compared the moment to Amazon's early Dynamo work, which began after engineers discovered that 70% of their 2004 database operations were simple key-value lookups.

That historical parallel is worth pausing on. Just as studying real traffic patterns led Amazon to simplify storage around key-value primitives, studying how agents fail reveals where verification environments fall short. Agents rarely fail because they cannot type syntax. They fail because they cannot observe realistic state transitions after each edit.

A November 15 engineering taxonomy titled Sandboxes for AI Agents by Shawn Mc arrives at the same conclusion from an operations perspective. Every agent sandbox forces a three-way tradeoff among realism, blast-radius safety, and startup speed or cost. Teams choose along a spectrum from lightweight single-service mocks, which Mc calls Island sandboxes, up to full isolated ephemeral stacks.

The practical problem with full ephemeral stacks is cold-start drag and cloud cost when an agent spawns twenty parallel branches. Conversely, naive mocks lie about IAM policies, rate limits, and eventual consistency. Official local emulators narrow that gap by delivering high-fidelity API behavior at local-process speed.

Without verifiable receipts from a safe test harness, faster code generation simply produces faster production incidents. An agent that writes Terraform in five seconds still needs a deterministic wall to bounce against before a human reviews the pull request. Receipts—logs, state diffs, and passing invariant checks—are what turn autonomous edits into mergeable engineering.

The builder lesson here is clear: invest in deterministic local emulators and invariant checks before scaling agent autonomy. Give every coding agent a fast, zero-blast-radius sandbox with explicit pre-flight assertions. That way, failed infrastructure mutations break locally in milliseconds rather than inside a shared staging account.

## DESK VI: The 0.55 NA Gamble: High-NA EUV Lithography and the Physics of Eight Nanometers

Follow that software stack all the way down to the silicon wafer, and you hit a physical wall inside a cleanroom. Every frontier model, agent sandbox, and inference cluster still depends on how tightly engineers can pack transistors onto a sliver of crystal. Today's documentary steps inside the world's most complex optical machine: ASML's High-NA extreme ultraviolet scanner.

Weighing roughly 165 tons and shipping in 250 crates across cargo jets and heavy trucks, each tool costs about $380 million. To understand why chipmakers are betting billions on this jump from 0.33 to 0.55 numerical aperture, we have to trace the physics from first principles. Let us begin with light, mirrors, and a seventy-year-old equation.

### Act I: The Diffraction Wall and Anamorphic Mirrors

Optical lithography obeys a simple rule known as the Rayleigh equation. The smallest feature you can print equals a process factor called k1, multiplied by the wavelength of light, divided by the numerical aperture of the lens. For half a century, chipmakers shrank features by marching down the electromagnetic spectrum. They moved from 365-nanometer mercury lamps to 248-nanometer krypton fluoride lasers, and finally to 193-nanometer argon fluoride immersion systems.

When 193-nanometer immersion hit a wall at a numerical aperture of 1.35, the industry made a radical leap to 13.5-nanometer extreme ultraviolet light. That transition took nearly three decades of coordinated research across national laboratories, industry consortia, and European optics houses. Shortening the wavelength fourteen-fold in one stroke bought another generation of scaling. Yet physics does not hand out free lunches. Because k1 cannot go below 0.25 without violating diffraction limits, even 13.5-nanometer light eventually hits a hard ceiling.

Standard Low-NA EUV scanners operate at a numerical aperture of 0.33, which gives a single-exposure resolution of about 13 nanometers. Once chipmakers push metal pitches below 20 nanometers, 0.33-NA tools can no longer print the pattern in one shot. Fabs have to split a single circuit layer into two or three separate masks. They run the wafer through the scanner multiple times and etch each pass with sub-nanometer alignment. Multipatterning works, but it multiplies cycle time, mask cost, and overlay error.

ASML's answer is High-NA EUV, embodied in the TWINSCAN EXE:5000 and EXE:5200 platforms. By raising the numerical aperture from 0.33 to 0.55, the scanner widens the cone of light captured from the mask. That sharpens single-exposure resolution by roughly forty percent, down to about eight nanometers. Suddenly, 19-nanometer and 20-nanometer pitch lines can print in a single pass. So why did it take more than a decade to widen that cone of light?

Here is the catch. At 13.5 nanometers, almost every material absorbs light, including glass lenses and air itself. EUV scanners cannot use refractive optics. Instead, they must bounce light inside a near-vacuum off ultra-flat Bragg mirrors built from dozens of alternating nanometer-thin layers of molybdenum and silicon. Even those engineered mirrors reflect only about seventy percent of the incoming beam. Worse still, if you simply widen the numerical aperture to 0.55 on a reflective photomask, the incoming light cone and the reflected outgoing cone collide.

To keep the beams from crashing into each other, Zeiss SMT engineers threw out symmetric optics altogether. They built anamorphic mirrors that demagnify the mask pattern by 4x along one axis and 8x along the other. That asymmetric squeeze keeps the incidence angles clean and prevents shadowing on the reflective mask. It also cuts the maximum single-exposure reticle field in half, shrinking it from 858 square millimeters to 429 square millimeters.

Think about what that half-field limit means for chip architects. Any processor larger than 26 by 16.5 millimeters can no longer print in a single flash. Large AI accelerators and server dies must be split across two mask fields and stitched together seamlessly on the wafer. Stage positioning has to hold within a fraction of a nanometer while scanning at high speed. In effect, Zeiss solved an impossible optical collision by handing chip designers and fab layout teams a brand-new geometric constraint.

Every extra stitching boundary demands careful floorplanning. Designers must route sensitive high-speed buses and clock trees cleanly across the seam so slight alignment tolerances never degrade signal timing. Even so, trading a half-field stitch for single-pass eight-nanometer resolution beats running a full wafer through three separate Low-NA exposures.

### Act II: Stochastic Photons, Tin Droplets, and Resist Chemistry

Even with anamorphic mirrors aligned to atomic tolerances, generating and controlling 13.5-nanometer light remains a brute-force feat. Inside the source vessel beneath the cleanroom floor, a high-power carbon dioxide laser blasts microscopic droplets of molten tin tens of thousands of times per second. Each droplet gets hit twice in midair. First, a pre-pulse flattens the sphere into a pancake target. Next, a main pulse vaporizes it into a superheated plasma radiating EUV photons. Most of that light never reaches silicon. Across roughly a dozen collector and projection mirrors, each bounce absorbs about thirty percent of the beam.

Once the surviving photons reach the wafer, a deeper quantum headache emerges. A single 13.5-nanometer photon carries roughly 92 electronvolts of energy, fourteen times more than a 193-nanometer deep-ultraviolet photon. Because energy scales inversely with wavelength, a fixed exposure dose delivers fourteen times fewer photons per square nanometer. At eight-nanometer dimensions, a transistor gate's fate comes down to a handful of discrete photon arrivals.

Physicists call this stochastic shot noise. Instead of a smooth, continuous wash of light, photons land like sparse raindrops on dry pavement. One tiny patch of photoresist receives slightly too few hits and leaves a broken copper line that kills the circuit. An adjacent patch catches a stray cluster and forms a microscopic bridge that shorts two neighboring wires together. Even when lines stay intact, random arrival gaps leave jagged edges known as line-edge roughness.

You cannot solve shot noise simply by cranking up exposure time, because slower wafer scans destroy fab throughput. Nor can you ignore how 92-electronvolt photons behave once they strike the resist. Each absorbed photon knocks loose cascades of secondary electrons that scatter sideways and blur the printed edge. Instead, the burden shifts to photoresist chemistry. Traditional chemically amplified resists struggle at single-digit nanometer scales because their acid molecules diffuse too far, and thin resist films tend to collapse during chemical development.

That is why the industry has pivoted toward metal-oxide negative-tone photoresists, known as MOR. Containing heavy metal atoms such as tin, these resists absorb 13.5-nanometer photons far more efficiently and resist blurring at tight pitches. Their inorganic core also gives them higher mechanical stiffness, which keeps tall, narrow lines from toppling over when rinse liquids evaporate. In August 2024, researchers at imec used High-NA EUV to pattern 9.5-nanometer logic lines at a 19-nanometer pitch, alongside 32-nanometer DRAM structures. Six months later, at SPIE Advanced Lithography in February 2025, imec cleared a bigger hurdle.

Using single-exposure High-NA EUV paired with metal-oxide resist, imec demonstrated greater than 90 percent electrical yield on 20-nanometer pitch metal lines. That result proved the optics and chemistry could deliver working electrical continuity across dense arrays, not just pretty microscope images. Still, turning a research yield milestone into millions of defect-free consumer chips requires conquering a second, harder bottleneck: fab economics.

Inside the source chamber, engineers also have to manage tin debris over thousands of operating hours. A gentle flow of hydrogen gas sweeps vaporized tin away from the fragile collector mirror so reflectivity does not degrade mid-run. Keeping that plasma stable day and night is what separates a laboratory demonstration from a commercial workhorse.

### Act III: The $380 Million Cleanroom Bet and Foundry Economics

Every High-NA EUV scanner carries a price tag near $380 million, roughly two to three times the cost of a standard Low-NA machine. Installing one requires reinforcing fab floors, carving out multi-story sub-fab bays for lasers and vacuum pumps, and assembling 250 crates over several months. Supply is gated not by ASML's final assembly bay in Veldhoven, but by two Zeiss SMT optical factories in Oberkochen and Wetzlar, Germany. Only those two sites on Earth can polish and coat the giant anamorphic mirrors. To ease that choke point, Zeiss is adding roughly 25,000 square meters of new cleanroom and production space.

When a single tool approaches $400 million installed, foundries face a brutal capital-allocation trade-off. Do you adopt High-NA EUV early to replace complex Low-NA multipatterning, or do you squeeze another node out of existing 0.33-NA tools whose capital costs are already depreciated? Single-exposure High-NA eliminates extra masks, saves weeks of fab cycle time, and cuts defect opportunities from repeated etch steps. Yet it pays off only if wafer throughput and uptime match proven Low-NA fleets.

Consider the math inside a high-volume fab. Every extra day a wafer spends cycling through double-patterning steps ties up expensive cleanroom capacity and delays revenue. On the other hand, if a $380 million High-NA scanner sits offline for mirror calibration or tin-source maintenance, depreciation still ticks away every hour. Tool availability and wafers per hour matter just as much as nanometer resolution.

On July 15, 2026, ASML and Intel confirmed that Intel Foundry had moved High-NA EUV into high-volume manufacturing at its D1X fab in Hillsboro, Oregon. The machines are patterning production layers on Intel 18A Panther Lake processors, sold commercially as Core Ultra Series 3 laptop chips. By taking delivery of the first commercial EXE systems, Intel bet that single-exposure simplicity and hands-on learning would outweigh the cost of pioneering a brand-new optical platform.

Rivals including TSMC and Samsung have taken a more measured path. They are extending Low-NA double patterning while waiting for the higher-throughput EXE:5200 generation and mature metal-oxide resists. Neither strategy is risk-free. Intel absorbs early-adopter teething pains, half-field stitching overhead on large dies, and steep depreciation charges up front. Late adopters avoid early tool downtime, yet they risk hitting a cycle-time and overlay wall when metal pitches tighten further.

This divergence also tests the broader supply chain. Photomask makers, pellicle suppliers, and metrology vendors must qualify new inspection tools tuned for anamorphic 4x and 8x geometries. No single scanner wins on its own; the entire ecosystem around the wafer has to mature in lockstep.

What we make of this gamble comes down to manufacturing discipline rather than optical brilliance. High-NA EUV has already cleared its core physics tests. It proved that 0.55-NA anamorphic mirrors and metal-oxide resists can print eight-nanometer features in one pass. From here, leadership in leading-edge silicon belongs to whichever foundry masters stochastic defect control, stitching precision, and wafers-per-hour throughput fastest—turning a $380 million marvel into boring, predictable yield.

### SEBI Prepares AI Usage Guidelines for Capital Markets as RBI Holds Microfinance Repayment Cap

India's financial regulators spent the weekend drawing clear guardrails around automated decision-making and household credit. SEBI Chairman Tuhin Kanta Pandey announced that the securities regulator will issue formal guidelines governing the use of artificial intelligence across capital markets. As brokerages, asset managers, and advisory platforms deploy machine-learning tools for trade execution, surveillance, and client profiling, Mumbai wants explicit lines of accountability.

Rather than banning automation, SEBI's framework aims to ensure that regulated intermediaries remain fully responsible for model outputs, audit trails, and investor protection. When an automated model misprices risk or generates flawed research, a human compliance officer must still answer for the failure. Firms will need clear testing logs and explainable controls before deploying models into live client workflows. Clear rules on disclosure and governance help legitimate engineering teams build without guessing where regulatory boundaries sit.

Across town at the Reserve Bank of India, policymakers reaffirmed an equally cautious stance on retail credit quality. The central bank confirmed that it will retain the existing loan repayment cap for microfinance institutions. Designed to limit how much of a low-income household's monthly earnings can go toward servicing unsecured debt, the cap acts as a circuit breaker against over-leverage.

Microfinance lenders have argued for looser ceilings as credit demand rebounds across semi-urban and rural districts, especially where household cash flows are recovering. ### Andhra Pradesh Converts 526 Investment Proposals Worth ₹17.1 Lakh Crore Into Projects, With 303 Grounded

Summit announcements are easy; land possession, power hookups, and environmental clearances are hard. Keeping the spotlight on grounded projects rather than headline pledges gives investors and local suppliers a far more honest yardstick of industrial momentum.

### Sixty Years After the 1966 Storm, the Leonid Meteor Shower Peaks This Week

Stargazers have a notable date on the calendar this week as the annual Leonid meteor shower reaches its peak on November 17 and 18. This year carries extra historical weight for astronomers and casual skywatchers alike. Sixty years ago, in November 1966, the Leonids erupted into one of the greatest meteor storms in modern memory, producing an estimated 144,000 meteors per hour across North American skies. Witnesses recalled shooting stars falling like snow.

Space.com notes that such an outburst happens only when Earth passes directly through a dense trail of debris shed by comet Tempel-Tuttle. Nobody expects a repeat of 1966's blizzard of light during this week's pass, as the thickest dust streams lie elsewhere along the orbit. Still, the Leonids remain famous for fast, bright streaks that leave glowing ionization trails behind for several seconds. Even a handful per hour feels electric in a dark sky.

Meanwhile, EarthSky's November night-sky guide points out that early risers and after-dark observers across mid-northern latitudes have plenty else to track. ### Keto Kitchen: Ghee-Roasted Andhra Kodi Vepudu Without the Rice

In the kitchen today, we are cooking Andhra Kodi Vepudu, a deeply savory, ghee-roasted spicy chicken fry that needs zero rice or starch on the side. Traditional restaurant versions often lean on refined seed oils, but strict-keto cooking starts with pure ghee. According to USDA FoodData Central figures, anhydrous butter oil or ghee delivers 100 grams of fat, zero carbohydrates, and 929 kilocalories per 100 grams. That gives the pan high-heat stability, clean fat, and unmistakable nutty aroma.

Start with bone-in or boneless chicken thighs, which stay juicy through a long dry roast and never turn chalky like lean breast meat. Marinate the chicken in turmeric, salt, and a measured spoonful of ginger-garlic paste. Keep the aromatics tight so carbohydrates stay low. Raw garlic carries 28.2 grams of carbs per 100 grams in USDA records, which works out to roughly 2.8 grams for a ten-gram clove portion. That leaves plenty of room in a daily carb budget.

Heat two to three tablespoons of ghee in a heavy-bottomed pan over medium flame, then add fresh curry leaves and slit green chillies until they crackle and turn fragrant. Drop in the marinated chicken and resist the urge to add water. As the meat cooks, its natural juices release, mingle with coriander powder and fiery Guntur red chilli powder, and slowly evaporate. Stir steadily so the spices toast evenly without scorching. Lower the heat during the final five minutes so the crust deepens without turning bitter.

That patience at the stove is what separates a true vepudu from a wet curry. By letting the moisture cook off completely, the ghee fries the spices directly onto the chicken into a dark, fragrant crust without any flour or starchy thickener. Serve it hot with a squeeze of lime and crisp cucumber slices on the edge of the plate, and you will not miss the rice for a second. Simple ingredients do all the heavy lifting.
