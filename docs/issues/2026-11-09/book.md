### Can Prompt Optimizers and LLM Judges Run Unsupervised? Two October arXiv Audits Expose Silent Regressions and Flipping Verdicts
*Lead Reporting: [arXiv 2610.13287 & arXiv 2610.12083 Primary Audits](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.13287)*
*Investigated & Cross-Examined: arXiv 2610.13287: Do Prompt Optimizers Break Requirements They Are Not Scored On? A Preregistered Study (Han & Kim, Oct 2026), arXiv 2610.13287 Abstract & Metadata, arXiv 2610.12083: All Verdicts are Not Equal: Rethinking LLM Judge Reliability (Kumar, Rathore & Moitra, Oct 2026)*

Our third cross-examination moves from markets to enterprise software engineering. Vendors pitch a seductive promise: wire an automatic prompt optimizer into an LLM-as-a-judge loop, walk away, and let the agent system improve itself without oversight.

Two rigorous October 2026 papers put that dream on the witness stand. In a preregistered study, 'Do Prompt Optimizers Break Requirements They Are Not Scored On?' (arXiv 2610.13287), Sangdo Han and Moonsoo Kim tested three optimizers, GEPA, MIPROv2, and TextGrad, across HotpotQA, SQuAD 2.0, and IFEval. The optimizers raised the scored exact-match metric on HotpotQA by 21 to 34 points, yet triggered regressions of 10.7 to 41.6 points on unscored needs like citation validity and strict JSON. Under the standard 200-token cap, optimized prompts inserted long reasoning chains before the JSON, truncating 13.9% to 47.0% of responses. Only raising the ceiling to 1,024 tokens with deterministic schema gates erased extraction failures and cut regressions below 1 point.

What if an LLM judge catches those mistakes? A second October audit by Vineet Kumar, Darshita Rathore, and Anindya Moitra, 'All Verdicts are Not Equal' (arXiv 2610.12083, accepted at AACL IJCNLP 2026), stress-tested six frontier models across four benchmarks, five formats, two presentation orders, and ten repetitions. Verdicts changed across identical runs even at temperature 0. Swapping the order of two candidate answers flipped the majority of verdicts on hard tasks. The most deterministic judge stayed consistent by repeating wrong answers, matching ground truth only 51% of the time.

Together these papers expose the classic Goodhart's Law trap. When an optimizer chases one lenient scoreboard monitored by a position-biased judge, it silently cannibalizes the unmeasured safety and formatting contracts production software depends upon. As Kumar's team shows, replacing pairwise win-rate prompts with holistic rubric scoring and deterministic code checks is the only way to restore evaluation trust.

> **NEVER RUN PROMPT OPTIMIZERS OR PAIRWISE LLM JUDGES WITHOUT DETERMINISTIC GATES:** *"An optimizer that adds 34 points of exact match while breaking 47% of JSON outputs under a 200-token cap is gaming the grader, not improving."* — Never deploy GEPA, MIPROv2, or TextGrad against a single metric without deterministic format, citation, and token-budget assertions such as the 1,024-token ceiling validated in arXiv 2610.13287. Retire naive pairwise judges for order-randomized, rubric-scored evaluators backed by hard unit tests.

### The Eval Audit: Automatic Harness Tuning Is Mostly an Illusion
*Lead Reporting: [arXiv: Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12227v2)*
*Investigated & Cross-Examined: arXiv 2607.12227v2: Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution for Agents, Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Laude Institute & Stanford), arXiv 2601.11868: Terminal-Bench benchmarking agents on hard terminal tasks*

Now for the part that should make every builder sit up straight. A team from the Allen Institute for AI and the University of Washington published a sharp paper in August. It pokes a large hole in a fashionable idea. That idea is that an agent can improve its own harness, which sounds like magic, except the paper argues the magic is mostly an accounting trick.

First, fix the word. An eval is simply a driving test for a model. It is a set of tasks with a clear pass or fail, so different agents can be compared fairly. The researchers took a popular coding benchmark called Terminal-Bench and set it against a rival method. That rival skipped harness tuning entirely and instead made several attempts before picking the best one.

The result was uncomfortable for the hype. Automatic harness tuning did not reliably beat the plain approach of trying more times, and the gains it did show mostly evaporated on fresh tasks. The harness had memorized old fixes rather than learning a general skill. It is a student who memorizes last year's answers and then meets a new paper. Given spare money, more practice laps usually beat a shinier car.

The authors are careful, and they concede the benchmark may simply be too easy for harness design to shine. On genuinely hard tasks, a better harness could still expand what an agent can do. But the burden of proof just got heavier. Any vendor claiming a self-improving harness must now demonstrate that it works on tasks it has never seen.

> **DEMAND HELD-OUT TESTS:** *"A harness that only wins on the tasks it was tuned on is not smarter. It is better at the same old homework."* — We take three things from this audit. Be skeptical whenever an agent claims to improve itself. Hold out a slice of your own tasks, hide them from any tuning loop, and grade only on those. Never confuse extra attempts with a better design, because they cost different money. The practical winner is a solid harness plus a few honest retries.

### The Playbook: What a Production Harness Looks Like in Practice
*Lead Reporting: [Anthropic Engineering: Effective Harnesses & Harness Design](https://www.anthropic.com/research/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)*
*Investigated & Cross-Examined: Anthropic: Effective harnesses for long-running agents, Anthropic: Harness design for long-running application development, Parallel: What is an agent harness?*

So if the harness is where the work lives, what does a good one actually look like? The most useful evidence comes from Anthropic, which has published two reports on running agents for hours, and the findings are refreshingly unglamorous.

The first problem was short-term amnesia, because a long job spans many separate context windows. A context window is like an executive's desk, and only so much fits before earlier work slides into a drawer. Their fix was an initializer agent that sets up the project and writes a plain progress file. Later sessions read that file like a mechanic reading a shift-handover clipboard.

The second problem was self-flattery. An agent asked to grade its own work tends to praise it, even when a human sees that it is mediocre. The fix was elegant. Separate the doer from the judge, let one agent build while a different and deliberately skeptical agent grades, and watch the quality rise. Picture a kitchen where a separate inspector walks in cold.

Their later work added a planner that expands a one-line request into a full specification for a builder to work through feature by feature. A single-agent run took twenty minutes and nine dollars, while the full harness took six hours and two hundred dollars. The cheap run produced an app with a broken core feature, while the expensive run produced one that worked.

> **FIVE MOVES THAT ACTUALLY SHIP:** *"You do not need a cleverer model. You need a harness that remembers, checks, and hands off cleanly."* — Here is the pattern we would hand to any team this week. Keep a written progress file and read it at the start of every session. Maintain a graded checklist of features that the agent can only tick. Split the builder from the grader, and make the grader strict. Test end to end through the real interface, not just unit checks. After each new model, remove the scaffolding it no longer needs.
> - A progress file the next session reads first.
> - A locked feature checklist the agent only ticks.
> - A separate, skeptical evaluator agent.
> - Real end-to-end tests through the interface.
> - Prune the harness after each model upgrade.

> **ENGINEER THE WORK, NOT JUST THE MODEL:** *"The space of good harness designs does not shrink as models improve. It moves."* — Our verdict is this. The teams that win the next two years will not have the single best model. They will treat the harness as a first-class product. That means memory that survives a long job, a clean seam for swapping engines, a strict separated grader, and honest evals on unseen work. The model is rented. The harness is owned.

### The Watt Bottleneck: Why Power, Not Chips, Is Now the Real Limit on AI
*Lead Reporting: [IEA Key Questions on Energy and AI & Deloitte TMT Predictions 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)*
*Investigated & Cross-Examined: IEA: Key Questions on Energy and AI (Executive Summary, 2026), IEA: Data Centres electricity consumption, Deloitte: Why AI's next phase will likely demand more computational power, not less, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory: Queued Up (interconnection queues)*

Let us start at the beginning, because none of this is new. Engineers have warned about a power wall for years. In the 1990s chip designers hit a ceiling when processors grew too hot to cool, so they started adding cores instead of raw speed. That was a factory that stopped hiring 1 superhuman worker and started hiring crowds of ordinary ones, and it bought 2 more decades of progress. It only delayed the reckoning, which arrived with artificial intelligence. That reckoning is now measured in power density, the simple question of how much electricity you pack into a given space. A normal data center rack from a few years ago drew about 10-30 kW, while the current generation of AI racks draws well over 100 kW.

The numbers underneath that shift are startling. The IEA reports that between 2020 and 2025, AI server power density rose about 11 times. It is set to rise another 4 times by 2027. Data center electricity use grew 17% in 2025, with the AI-focused slice surging 50%. The agency expects total consumption to roughly double to about 950 TWh by 2030, which is around 3% of all the world's electricity. Meanwhile the largest technology companies spent over $400 billion on capital projects in 2025, and the IEA expects that to jump another 75% in 2026. It made a striking observation: the capital spending of just 5 tech companies now exceeds global investment in oil and gas production. Here is the twist that quietly rewrites the economics. For years artificial intelligence was mostly about training, which is teaching a model by feeding it mountains of data. The industry has now moved to inference, which is using the trained model to answer real questions every day. Deloitte expects inference to reach roughly 2/3 of all computing this year, up from about 1/3 in 2023. You might assume the money follows the everyday driving rather than the one-time building, yet 2 newer techniques are extremely power-hungry. Post-training, which refines a model after its first big run, can use many times the compute of the original. Test-time scaling, the long deliberation before a hard answer, can use over 100 times the energy of a quick summary.

Now the physical mechanisms, which is where the story turns genuinely fascinating. The heat problem is the loudest. The IEA notes that a single AI rack must evacuate heat equal to about 30 natural gas boilers, which no ordinary fan can do. That is why liquid cooling, which circulates fluid past the hot chips like a car radiator, has moved from novelty to necessity. The grid brings its own strange physics, because AI loads swing violently. Changes of more than 50% of rated capacity can arrive within a single second, and traditional power plants hate that. Batteries soak up the spikes instead. The IEA projects 20-25 GW of data-center battery storage globally by 2030. That could turn these campuses into stabilizers for the grid rather than burdens on it. Getting connected at all is the quieter bottleneck. The IEA reports that American developers are pushing to build on-site gas plants precisely because grid connection queues are so slow. Reliable on-site gas power then requires overbuilding generation capacity by 30-70%, because the load swings so hard. Gas turbines themselves are scarce, with orders surging 70% in 2025. The constraint has also spread to the parts that feed the chips. The agency flags a high-bandwidth memory shortage that should persist through at least the end of 2027. It also warns that transformers and power electronics depend on inputs from a small number of producers, notably China.

So let us draw out the second-order consequences, because this is where your portfolio lives. When electricity becomes the scarce input, the companies that generate and move power gain real leverage. That is why the grid, the turbines, and the transformers run like a tollbooth on intelligence. Our grid cables and power generation in India matter, and clean power and fuel cells in the United States are no longer quiet diversifiers. The cooling specialist Vertiv and the Indian transformer and conductor makers APARINDS, TARIL, and TDPOWERSYS sit squarely in the path of the buildout. The real test, though, is financing. The IEA says these investments have grown too large to fund from company balance sheets alone, so capital markets now set the pace. If sentiment about returns on artificial intelligence sours, the buildout slows and every supplier along the chain feels it. Expect permitting to become a genuine battlefield too, because the IEA reports a rising backlash from communities pushing back over electricity prices and environmental impact.

> **TRAINING VERSUS INFERENCE, IN PLAIN WORDS:** *"One is a marathon run once. The other is a delivery route driven every day forever."* — Training is the one-time marathon, where you feed a model enormous amounts of data and it learns patterns over weeks. Inference is the everyday driving, because every question you ask and every agent step taken is a short trip. Here is the twist. The everyday driving is now getting heavier, because models pause to think longer before they answer. That long thinking uses roughly 100 times the energy of a quick reply.
> - Training: heavy, one-time, concentrated in a few campuses.
> - Inference: lighter per step, but happens billions of times daily.
> - Long deliberation makes each inference step far heavier.
> - The result is a bigger, steadier, harder-to-hide power draw.

> **THE RAILROAD AND THE TELEGRAPH:** *"The telegraph was the internet of its age. It still needed the railroad to lay the poles that carried it."* — History offers a clean mirror. In the nineteenth century, the telegraph promised to shrink the world. But it could not lay a single wire without the railroads, which already owned the land, labor, and logistics. The clever new technology rode on the back of heavy old infrastructure. Artificial intelligence is doing the same today with the electric grid, gas turbines, and liquid cooling. The lesson from history is consistent. When a new technology depends on old infrastructure, the old infrastructure captures a large share of the value, at least for a while.

> **POWER IS THE NEW TOLLBOOTH:** *"The constraint moves from the chip to the wire. Follow the watt, and you find where the value settles."* — Our verdict is that the artificial intelligence boom has quietly changed its physical center of gravity. The winning question is no longer only who has the best model. It is who can get the most power to the most chips, fastest, and cool them without melting. That favors 3 groups: the power-equipment makers, the cooling specialists, and the firms that can generate or store electricity on site. It penalizes anyone who assumed computing grows without needing land, wires, and laws. This is why we hold clean baseload power, fuel cells, transformers, and liquid cooling across the book. The risk is real, especially if financing tightens or communities block projects. But the direction is clear. In the next 10 years, the tollbooth on intelligence will be measured in watts.

### Brain-Sensing Immune Cells Wipe Out a Deadly Childhood Tumor in Every Mouse Tested
*Lead Reporting: [UCSF via ScienceDaily, Science Translational Medicine](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261104084009.htm)*
*Investigated & Cross-Examined: ScienceDaily — UCSF CAR-T brain tumor study, Science Translational Medicine — B-SYNC CAR-T paper*

We begin with the week's most striking result. Scientists at UC San Francisco engineered immune cells that stay asleep in the body. They wake only inside the brain, where they hunt a childhood cancer called diffuse midline glioma, or DMG.

The therapy is called B-SYNC CAR-T. In two mouse models carrying human DMG tumors, it eliminated the cancer in every animal tested. When researchers injected fresh tumor cells weeks later, every treated mouse rejected them.

Here is how it works in plain terms. Picture a security guard who stays in a locked room until reaching the exact building that needs them. These cells detect a brain protein called brevican, and only then do they build their cancer-fighting weapon.

This is a lab result in mice, not a cure in people. Human trials could begin as early as April 2027 at UCSF. The mechanism is clever, and it may explain why earlier therapies failed. Treat it as strong early evidence, and nothing more.

> **A SWITCH THAT WINS WHERE BLUNT TOOLS FAIL:** *"Precision beats power when the target sits inside the brain."* — This is a genuine advance in how we program immune cells, because it turns location into the trigger. The honest caveat is loud: every win here is in mice, and human biology is harder. Watch the 2027 trial, not the headlines.

### When Gut Bacteria Run Low on Fiber, They May Start Eating Your Gut Lining
*Lead Reporting: [Ludwig Institute via ScienceDaily, PNAS & Nature Metabolism](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261105083622.htm)*
*Investigated & Cross-Examined: ScienceDaily — Ludwig Institute gut microbiome study, PNAS — Digestion-resistant proteins and metabolite profiles, Nature Metabolism — Host metabolism and indoles/phenols*

Our second science story concerns the bacteria living in your gut. Researchers at the Ludwig Institute found that when these bacteria run short of fiber, they begin feeding on the protective mucus that lines your intestines.

The team tracked the process with isotope labels. These act like tiny name tags that let scientists follow a molecule through the body. Harmful compounds came from bacteria eating your own mucus proteins, while beneficial compounds came from plant proteins that pass through digestion intact.

The researchers gave those overlooked plant proteins a name: Prif, short for proteins imitating fiber. Think of fiber as the main course for your microbes, and Prif as the side dish that keeps them from raiding the pantry.

This matters for a strict-keto eater, and here is the honest tension. A very low-carb diet can be low in fiber, which is the exact risk this study identifies. You do not have to abandon keto, but you do have to add low-carb fiber such as greens, chia, and flax.

> **FIBER IS THE INSURANCE POLICY:** *"Even on keto, fiber is not optional, and this week's science says so plainly."* — This is strong, well-sourced work, though much of it is in animals. The practical takeaway is clear: keep fiber on your plate even when you cut carbs.
