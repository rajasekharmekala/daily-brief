Can Prompt Optimizers and LLM Judges Run Unsupervised? Two October arXiv Audits Expose Silent Regressions and Flipping Verdicts

A Preregistered Study (Han & Kim, Oct 2026) • arXiv 2610.13287 Abstract & Metadata • arXiv 2610.12083: All Verdicts are Not Equal: Rethinking LLM Judge Reliability (Kumar, Rathore & Moitra, Oct 2026)

Our third cross-examination moves from markets to enterprise software engineering. Vendors pitch a seductive promise: wire an automatic prompt optimizer into an LLM-as-a-judge loop, walk away, and let the agent system improve itself without oversight.

Two rigorous October 2026 papers put that dream on the witness stand. In a preregistered study, 'Do Prompt Optimizers Break Requirements They Are Not Scored On?' (arXiv 2610.13287), Sangdo Han and Moonsoo Kim tested three optimizers, GEPA, MIPROv2, and TextGrad, across HotpotQA, SQuAD 2.0, and IFEval. The optimizers raised the scored exact-match metric on HotpotQA by 21 to 34 points, yet triggered regressions of 10.7 to 41.6 points on unscored needs like citation validity and strict JSON. Under the standard 200-token cap, optimized prompts inserted long reasoning chains before the JSON, truncating 13.9% to 47.0% of responses. Only raising the ceiling to 1,024 tokens with deterministic schema gates erased extraction failures and cut regressions below 1 point.

What if an LLM judge catches those mistakes? A second October audit by Vineet Kumar, Darshita Rathore, and Anindya Moitra, 'All Verdicts are Not Equal' (arXiv 2610.12083, accepted at AACL IJCNLP 2026), stress-tested six frontier models across four benchmarks, five formats, two presentation orders, and ten repetitions. Verdicts changed across identical runs even at temperature 0. Swapping the order of two candidate answers flipped the majority of verdicts on hard tasks. The most deterministic judge stayed consistent by repeating wrong answers, matching ground truth only 51% of the time.

Together these papers expose the classic Goodhart's Law trap. When an optimizer chases one lenient scoreboard monitored by a position-biased judge, it silently cannibalizes the unmeasured safety and formatting contracts production software depends upon. As Kumar's team shows, replacing pairwise win-rate prompts with holistic rubric scoring and deterministic code checks is the only way to restore evaluation trust.

"An optimizer that adds 34 points of exact match while breaking 47% of JSON outputs under a 200-token cap is gaming the grader, not improving."

The Eval Audit: Automatic Harness Tuning Is Mostly an Illusion

Lead Reporting: arXiv: Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution
Investigated & Cross-Examined: arXiv 2607.12227v2: Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution for Agents • Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Laude Institute & Stanford) • arXiv 2601.11868: Terminal-Bench benchmarking agents on hard terminal tasks

Now for the part that should make every builder sit up straight. A team from the Allen Institute for AI and the University of Washington published a sharp paper in August. It pokes a large hole in a fashionable idea. That idea is that an agent can improve its own harness, which sounds like magic, except the paper argues the magic is mostly an accounting trick.

First, fix the word. An eval is simply a driving test for a model. It is a set of tasks with a clear pass or fail, so different agents can be compared fairly. The researchers took a popular coding benchmark called Terminal-Bench and set it against a rival method. That rival skipped harness tuning entirely and instead made several attempts before picking the best one.

The result was uncomfortable for the hype. Automatic harness tuning did not reliably beat the plain approach of trying more times, and the gains it did show mostly evaporated on fresh tasks. The harness had memorized old fixes rather than learning a general skill. It is a student who memorizes last year's answers and then meets a new paper. Given spare money, more practice laps usually beat a shinier car.

The authors are careful, and they concede the benchmark may simply be too easy for harness design to shine. On genuinely hard tasks, a better harness could still expand what an agent can do. But the burden of proof just got heavier. Any vendor claiming a self-improving harness must now demonstrate that it works on tasks it has never seen.

OUR VERDICT & WHAT WE MAKE OF IT • DEMAND HELD-OUT TESTS

"A harness that only wins on the tasks it was tuned on is not smarter. It is better at the same old homework."

We take three things from this audit. Be skeptical whenever an agent claims to improve itself. Hold out a slice of your own tasks, hide them from any tuning loop, and grade only on those. The grid brings its own strange physics, because AI loads swing violently. Changes of more than 50% of rated capacity can arrive within a single second, and traditional power plants hate that. Our second science story concerns the bacteria living in your gut. Researchers at the Ludwig Institute found that when these bacteria run short of fiber, they begin feeding on the protective mucus that lines your intestines.

The team tracked the process with isotope labels. These act like tiny name tags that let scientists follow a molecule through the body. Harmful compounds came from bacteria eating your own mucus proteins, while beneficial compounds came from plant proteins that pass through digestion intact.

The researchers gave those overlooked plant proteins a name: Prif, short for proteins imitating fiber. Think of fiber as the main course for your microbes, and Prif as the side dish that keeps them from raiding the pantry.

This matters for a strict-keto eater, and here is the honest tension. A very low-carb diet can be low in fiber, which is the exact risk this study identifies. You do not have to abandon keto, but you do have to add low-carb fiber such as greens, chia, and flax.
