The Eval Audit: Automatic Harness Tuning Is Mostly an Illusion

Lead Reporting: arXiv: Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution
Investigated & Cross-Examined: arXiv 2607.12227v2: Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution for Agents • Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Laude Institute & Stanford) • arXiv 2601.11868: Terminal-Bench benchmarking agents on hard terminal tasks

Now for the part that should make every builder sit up. A team from the Allen Institute for AI and the University of Washington published a sharp paper in August. It pokes a hole in a fashionable idea. The idea is that an agent can improve its own harness. It sounds like magic. The paper says the magic is mostly an accounting trick.

First, fix the term. An eval is simply a driving test for a model. It is a set of tasks with a clear pass or fail, so you can compare agents fairly. The researchers took a popular coding benchmark called Terminal-Bench and tested a rival method. Instead of tuning the harness, that rival simply made several attempts and picked the best one.

The result was uncomfortable for the hype. Automatic harness tuning did not reliably beat the simple approach of trying more times. Here is why that matters. If you have extra money, you can polish the car, or you can take more practice laps. The paper found that more laps usually won. Worse, the tuning gains mostly evaporated on fresh tasks. The harness had memorized old fixes rather than learning a general skill. Think of a student who memorizes last year's answers. Brilliant on that paper, useless on a new one.

The authors are careful. They note the benchmark may simply be too easy for harness design to shine. On hard enough tasks, a better harness genuinely could expand what an agent can do. But the burden of proof just got heavier. Any vendor claiming a self-improving harness must show it works on tasks it has never seen.

OUR VERDICT & WHAT WE MAKE OF IT • DEMAND HELD-OUT TESTS

"A harness that only wins on the tasks it was tuned on is not smarter. It is better at the same old homework."

We take three things from this audit. First, be skeptical whenever an agent claims to improve itself. Second, hold out a slice of your own tasks, hide them from any tuning loop, and grade only on those. Third, do not confuse extra attempts with a better design, because they cost different money. The practical winner is a solid harness plus a few honest retries.

So if the harness is where the work lives, what does a good one look like? The most useful evidence comes from Anthropic, which has published two reports on running agents for hours. The findings are refreshingly unglamorous.

The Watt Bottleneck: Why Power, Not Chips, Is Now the Real Limit on AI

Lead Reporting: IEA Key Questions on Energy and AI & Deloitte TMT Predictions 2026
Investigated & Cross-Examined: IEA: Key Questions on Energy and AI (Executive Summary, 2026) • IEA: Data Centres electricity consumption • Deloitte: Why AI's next phase will likely demand more computational power, not less • Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory: Queued Up (interconnection queues)

Let us start with the backstory, because none of this is new. Engineers have warned about a power wall for years. In the 1990s, chip designers hit a ceiling when processors grew too hot to cool. They fixed it by adding cores instead of raw speed. It was like a factory that stopped hiring one superhuman worker and started hiring a hundred ordinary ones. That trick bought two more decades. It just delayed the reckoning.

The modern reckoning arrived with artificial intelligence, and it is measured in something called power density. That is simply how much electricity you pack into a given space. A normal data center rack from a few years ago drew maybe ten to thirty kilowatts. The current generation of AI racks draws well over a hundred. The IEA notes that between 2020 and 2025, AI server power density rose about eleven times. It is set to rise another four times by 2027. Picture a kitchen where you keep swapping in ever-larger stoves, but never widen the counter.

Now the demand picture. The IEA reports that data center electricity use grew seventeen percent in 2025, with the AI-focused slice surging fifty percent. It expects data center consumption to roughly double to about nine hundred and fifty terawatt-hours by 2030. That is around three percent of all the world's electricity. Meanwhile the largest technology companies spent over four hundred billion dollars on capital projects in 2025. The IEA expects that to jump another seventy-five percent in 2026. It made a striking observation. The capital spending of just five tech companies now exceeds global investment in oil and gas production.

This leads to the first great bottleneck, the one nobody outside the energy world talks about. It is the grid connection queue. Before a data center can draw power, it must get permission and a physical hookup from the grid operator. That line is brutally long. The IEA reports that American data center developers are pushing to build their own on-site gas plants. They do this precisely because grid connections are so slow. It notes that supplying reliable on-site gas power requires overbuilding generation capacity by thirty to seventy percent, because the load swings so violently. And gas turbines themselves are now scarce, with orders surging seventy percent in 2025.

Here is the second shift, and it is the one that quietly rewrites the economics of the entire industry. For years, artificial intelligence was mostly about training. Training is teaching a model by feeding it mountains of data, like a student cramming for a final exam. Now the industry has moved to inference, which is simply using the trained model to answer real questions every day. Think of training as building the car and inference as all the driving it will ever do. Deloitte expects inference to reach roughly two-thirds of all computing this year, up from about a third in 2023.

You might think the money follows the everyday driving and not the one-time building. That is only half true. Deloitte makes a subtler and more important point. New techniques called post-training and test-time scaling are extremely power-hungry. Post-training, which refines a model after its first big training run, can use many times the compute of the original. Test-time scaling, the long deliberation a model does before answering a hard question, can use over a hundred times the energy of a simple summary. So the shift to inference does not reduce the power appetite. It multiplies it in a new shape.

Now the physical mechanisms, because this is where the story gets fascinating. The heat problem is the loudest. The IEA notes a single AI rack must evacuate heat equal to about thirty natural gas boilers. You cannot cool that with ordinary fans. That is why liquid cooling, which circulates fluid right past the hot chips like a car's radiator, has moved from novelty to necessity. This is exactly why companies that make liquid cooling systems, like our holding in Vertiv, have become infrastructure plays rather than side bets.

The second mechanism is the electricity grid itself, and its strange new physics. AI loads swing violently, the IEA says, with changes of more than fifty percent of rated capacity within one second. That is like a city where everyone flips their stove on and off together, every second, all day. Traditional power plants and grids hate this. The answer is storage, meaning big batteries that soak up the spikes and smooth them out. The IEA projects twenty to twenty-five gigawatts of battery storage installed inside data centers globally by 2030. Notably, that could turn data centers into a stabilizer for the grid rather than a burden on it.

And then there is the third mechanism, the one that ties directly to the semiconductor theme running through our portfolio. The IEA flags a shortage of high-bandwidth memory, the specialized memory glued right next to the AI chips. Think of it as the chip's scratch pad, and it is the bottleneck inside the bottleneck. The agency expects this shortage to persist through at least the end of 2027. It also warns that advanced power electronics and transformers depend on critical inputs from a small number of producers, notably China. In other words, the constraint has spread from the chip to the sub-components that feed the chip.

Brain-Sensing Immune Cells Wipe Out a Deadly Childhood Tumor in Every Mouse Tested

Lead Reporting: UCSF via ScienceDaily, Science Translational Medicine
Investigated & Cross-Examined: ScienceDaily — UCSF CAR-T brain tumor study • Science Translational Medicine — B-SYNC CAR-T paper

Let us begin with the most striking result of the week. Scientists at UC San Francisco engineered immune cells that stay asleep in the body and wake up only inside the brain. Once awake, they hunt a childhood brain cancer called diffuse midline glioma, or DMG.

The treatment is called B-SYNC CAR-T. In two mouse models carrying human DMG tumors, it eliminated the cancer in every animal tested. When researchers injected new tumor cells weeks later, every treated mouse rejected them.

Here is how it works, in plain terms. Imagine a security guard who stays in a locked room until they reach the exact building that needs them. These immune cells detect a brain protein called brevican, and only then do they build their cancer-fighting weapon. Think of it as a switch only the brain can flip.

This is a lab result in mice, not a cure in people. Human trials could begin as early as April 2027 at UCSF. The mechanism is clever, and it may solve why earlier therapies failed. Treat this as strong early evidence, and nothing more.

When Gut Bacteria Run Low on Fiber, They May Start Eating Your Gut Lining

Lead Reporting: Ludwig Institute via ScienceDaily, PNAS & Nature Metabolism
Investigated & Cross-Examined: ScienceDaily — Ludwig Institute gut microbiome study • PNAS — Digestion-resistant proteins and metabolite profiles • Nature Metabolism — Host metabolism and indoles/phenols

Our second science story concerns the bacteria living in your gut. Researchers at the Ludwig Institute found that when these bacteria run short of fiber, they begin feeding on the protective mucus that lines your intestines.

The team tracked the process with isotope labels, which act like tiny name tags that let scientists follow a molecule through the body. Harmful compounds came from bacteria eating your own mucus proteins. Beneficial compounds came almost entirely from plant proteins that pass through digestion intact.

The researchers gave those overlooked plant proteins a name. They call them Prif, short for proteins imitating fiber. Think of fiber as the main course for your microbes, and Prif as the side dish that keeps them from raiding the pantry.

This matters for a strict-keto eater, and here is the honest tension. A very low-carb diet can be low in fiber, which is the exact risk this study identifies. You do not have to abandon keto. You do have to add low-carb fiber, such as greens, chia, and flax.

Start with two hundred grams of crumbled paneer. Heat two tablespoons of ghee or butter in a pan. Add cumin seeds, green chillies, and curry leaves. Then add a handful of gongura leaves, or a spoon of gongura pickle if fresh leaves are hard to find.
