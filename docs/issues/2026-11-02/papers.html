Trending agent papers

arXiv / Hugging Face Daily Papers: Oct 19 · 6m
Deep Search in Real-World Web Environments  —  OpenDS-World tests live-web research across 200 tasks with 12 frontier models under one tool protocol. The best-performing model reached 39.0% accuracy, while human evaluation across 1,000 independent attempts reached 21.1%. The authors also report persistent challenges in tool coordination and long-horizon search management. These are benchmark results, not evidence that the task suite captures every useful research task.
Takeaway: For your long-horizon agent and evaluation interests, this benchmark tests evidence gathering on the live web rather than static retrieval.
arXiv / Hugging Face Daily Papers: Oct 22 · 6m
IQuest-Q1: Advancing Foundation Capabilities for Agentic CLI Systems  —  IQuest-Q1 is an open sparse mixture-of-experts foundation model for agentic coding, with 320B total parameters and 15B activated. The paper describes a three-stage training process and merging experts/checkpoints. The abstract reports coding and general-task capabilities but provides no benchmark scores, so its model size and training recipe do not establish a comparative capability advantage.
Takeaway: Your production-agent paper tracking includes agentic CLI systems; this is a widely surfaced model release to compare against harness-level results.
arXiv preprint: 3 days ago · 5m
Search When Needed, Proceed When Reliable: Metacognitive Reliability Supervision for Efficient Agentic Search  —  This paper trains agents to estimate whether their current reasoning is reliable enough to answer or whether search is needed, then separately scores whether retrieved evidence improves that reliability. Those signals form a process reward for GRPO rather than relying only on final correctness or a whole-trajectory score. The authors report a better accuracy-efficiency trade-off than strong agentic-search baselines across seven question-answering benchmarks. The abstract gives no effect sizes, and says code and checkpoints will be open-sourced, so independent reproduction remains a useful next check. No popularity signal shown.
Takeaway: This is directly relevant to your agent-evaluation and tool-use interests because it makes the search-versus-stop decision an explicit reliability target.
arXiv preprint: 4 days ago · 5m
Long-Term Agent Memory through Complementary Curation and Consolidation  —  The authors combine episodic memories with structured relational memories and add a consolidation mechanism intended to preserve efficiency. On LoCoMo and LongMemEval-S, the hybrid system reports 90.2% and 85.5% accuracy, respectively, and says the memory types are complementary to one another. The abstract compares the result favorably with existing frameworks, but does not identify all costs or benchmark-specific failure modes; the full paper is needed before treating those scores as a general long-term-memory result. No popularity signal shown.
Takeaway: This is closely tied to your long-horizon agent and memory interests; the two benchmark scores offer a concrete starting point for a reproduction comparison.
arXiv preprint: 4 days ago · 5m
More Rules, Longer Lists? Benchmarking Self-Evolving Agents on a Hidden-Rule Curriculum  —  CLR-Bench tests whether self-evolving agents infer hidden rules or merely append each correction as another exception. It contains 20 curriculum chains across 10 domains and compares six self-evolution methods. With a certified model, the best methods improve by at most 5 points over a frozen agent and exclude only 6–12% of unseen members; retaining raw practice history excludes 20%. A fixed lesson about how to interpret the grouping raises results by 14.5–16.5 points. This is a narrow synthetic curriculum, but it exposes a concrete way memory growth can imitate learning. No popularity signal shown.
Takeaway: Your agent-memory and harness interests make this a useful test of whether persistent rules generalize or just accumulate exceptions.
arXiv preprint: 3 days ago · 5m
Counterfactual Pairwise Augmentation Improves Small Pointwise LLM Judges  —  CPA creates and audits an opposite-label answer for each pointwise judging example, trains on both the original and pairwise views, then deploys the judge pointwise. On Qwen3-8B LoRA adapters for six binary criteria, the authors report a 2.5-point gain in average pointwise macro-F1 over pointwise-only training and better results than model merging on all six criteria at each tested merge weight. It exceeds the paper’s zero-shot Gemini 3.6 Flash judge by 11.8 points. The evidence is from this six-criterion setup; broader evaluator domains remain untested. No popularity signal shown.
Takeaway: Your LLM-as-judge and evaluation interests make the training setup useful to watch, especially its test of criterion-level decision boundaries.

Runtime, harness and production engineering

arXiv preprint: Oct 23 · 6m
When Agents Fail: Model or Harness--Who's to Blame?  —  This study argues that agent performance belongs to a model–harness pairing, then separates valid completion, model–harness fit, cross-model fit, interaction effects, and token load. It evaluates four general-purpose harnesses with four models across six benchmarks; 111 settings produce 52,168 trajectories. The authors find that rankings can change by model and benchmark, and that task score may diverge from whether a run completes in an evaluable form. The study covers selected public systems and benchmarks, so it supports interaction-aware comparisons rather than a universal harness ranking.
Takeaway: This supports your production-agent harness work by separating execution completion from answer correctness and model–runtime fit.
arXiv preprint: Oct 18 · 6m
Design and Cross-Domain Evaluation of an Agent Execution Harness  —  A planner, scoped workers, and a reviewer share an objective while keeping plan and execution record separate. The authors report 83 of 89 Terminal-Bench 2.1 instances passed (93.26%) and a length-adjusted HealthBench Professional score of 69.63 over 525 tasks. In a 53-task paired HealthBench comparison the aggregate score rose 19.89 points, while the median task changed by −1.46 points and mean inference cost rose from $0.29 to $2.13 per task. The instruction set was refined without official grading feedback, making the reported cross-domain gains an author-run result to verify independently.
Takeaway: Your harness and agent-evaluation interests make the cost and task-level spread as important as the headline pass rate.
arXiv preprint: Oct 15 · 6m
JevAgentHarness: Benchmarking, Deploying and Training Decision Models That Share Control with LLMs  —  JevAgentHarness delegates routine multiple-choice decisions to a smaller decision model but fits a confidence threshold on the agent’s own trajectories, returning uncertain choices to the larger model. In the authors’ benchmark, the strongest isolated decision model solved only 69.5% of agent tasks, and uncalibrated tool-choice delegation reduced success by 19.0 points. Their AgentDecision-4B reached 91.5% on tasks unlike its training data, versus 82.0% for Jev. These are task-set results, not a guarantee of cheaper production inference; the abstract does not quantify end-to-end savings across deployments.
Takeaway: This is relevant to your runtime and cost-control interests because it shows why delegated decisions need trajectory-aware confidence gates.
arXiv preprint: Oct 19 · 6m
HarnessPro: Recursive Harness Self-Improvement with Pareto Optimization for Performance-Cost Trade-offs  —  HarnessPro evolves multiple candidate harnesses and returns cost-focused, balanced, or performance-focused configurations instead of one presumed best setup. It uses LLM-generated tests without ground-truth solutions or official tests, then feeds execution results into the next iteration. Across Terminal-Bench 2, SWE-Bench Pro, and ProgramBench, the heavy GPT-5.5-with-Codex mode reports an average pass-rate gain of 2.0 percentage points and average per-task cost reduction of 26.1% relative to RHO; the paper says the advantage also holds with GLM-5.2 and Claude Code. The self-generated test signal is an important limit to audit.
Takeaway: Your prompt-optimization and harness interests make this measured performance–cost trade-off worth tracking; inspect its verifier design before adopting the recipe.
arXiv preprint: Oct 13 · 6m
Tev: Scaling Long-Horizon Agentic Environments with Dense Progress Rewards  —  Tev-Gym supplies long-horizon training environments with verifiable intermediate progress instead of waiting for sparse end rewards: 1,170 environments across seven domains. Its progress-potential reward shaping recipe trains Tev-27B, which the authors say matches GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5 at Pass@5 on Agents’ Last Exam computing and math domains. They also report transfer to Terminal-Bench subsets. The abstract labels these comparisons substantial but does not provide numerical scores, so the magnitude and benchmark details need checking in the paper tables.
Takeaway: Your long-horizon agent interests make the intermediate-reward design relevant, particularly for evaluating whether progress signals improve task completion.

Evaluation, observability and safety

arXiv preprint: 4 days ago · 5m
VeriCUA: Verification as Agentic Exploration for Computer-Using Agents  —  VeriCUA treats task verification as evidence gathering: after building a rubric, the verifier chooses GUI or code checks for unresolved requirements and combines their findings under explicit evidence rules. It aims to work across online and offline settings, platforms, and observation modes, addressing the partial view of screenshot judges and state-inspection scripts. The authors report better agreement with reference labels across settings, largest gains on hard offline cases, and stronger rejection of false completion claims online. The abstract gives no effect sizes and labels the work in progress, so the size and stability of the gain need follow-up.
Takeaway: Your trace-evaluation and computer-use interests make this a useful design to compare with screenshot-only or state-only completion checks.
arXiv preprint: Oct 19 · 5m
VerifierBench: Benchmarking the Generation of Executable Verifiers for LLM Agents  —  VerifierBench measures whether an agent can write a verifier that accepts every valid solution and rejects invalid ones. It includes 222 tasks from six benchmarks and 4,993 hidden tests, including alternative correct solutions and attacks; a six-check V4V audit screens the source tests and verifiers. Across 19 models, normalized balanced accuracy ranges from 10.6 to 62.1. Generated verifiers reject invalid outputs more reliably than they accept valid ones, often because they reject every tested solution. This makes valid-solution coverage a key audit dimension, though the benchmark’s task mix is still bounded.
Takeaway: This is relevant to your evaluation and guardrail interests because it treats verifier false rejection as a first-class reliability failure.
arXiv preprint: Oct 21 · 5m
What the Trace Shows and What the Label Says: Auditing Behavioral Detectors in Agent Evaluation  —  This study audits three trace-level detectors in a calculator-use setup with deliberately corrupted outputs, six models, and 30 problems per model. Among 90 human-reviewed traces, automated labels matched all reviewed cases for final correctness, fault impact, and fault relevance, but only 67 of 81 for verification. A silent tool-output override can let a model recover without visibly rechecking, confusing a detector that equates re-verification with recovery. Step and token limits also moved one model from 21/30 to 27/30 correct. The setup offers clear ground truth but is narrow to a calculator task.
Takeaway: Your trace-evaluation interests make its distinction between an automated label and the evidence visible in a trajectory especially useful.
