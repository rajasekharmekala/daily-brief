Voice AI Latency Physics: Inside Real-Time Turn-Taking, Streaming ASR, and Neural TTS Pipelines

How conversational agents budget the 200-millisecond human turn gap across WebRTC, acoustic endpointing, LLM first tokens, and speech-to-speech token economics.

Executive Takeaways

  • Human conversation turns peak between 0 and 200 ms across languages, while well-tuned cascaded WebRTC voice agents spend roughly 750 to 950 ms per turn—mostly on endpointing wait (~300 ms) and LLM time-to-first-token (~300 ms), with framework overhead accounting for roughly 2%.
  • Endpointing architecture is the central design split of 2026: Deepgram Flux embeds turn detection inside the STT recognizer (~260 ms p50), LiveKit Turn Detector v1.0 fuses acoustic and semantic branches at the framework layer (9.9% false cutoffs at a 300 ms budget), and Pipecat Smart Turn v3 runs an 8 MB int8 ONNX model on CPU in 12 ms—yet academic evaluations show learned detectors still struggle with recall and premature responses.
  • Native speech-to-speech models compress raw audio latency, but Sierra and Princeton's τ-Voice benchmark shows full-duplex voice agents retain only 30–45% of text reasoning accuracy (26–38% pass@1 under realistic noise and accents vs. 85% in text) unless conversational timing is decoupled from semantic planning.
  • Headline per-minute rates mislead on speech-to-speech APIs because uncached turns re-read growing session histories: a 5-minute 12-turn call on Gemini 3.8 Live costs ~$0.038/min (~3x its raw audio meter), while OpenAI's gpt-realtime-2.1 ranges from $0.06–$0.11/min with prompt caching to $1.17 for that same 5-minute call uncached.
Strategic Context: Ties directly to your active interest in interactive real-time voice agents, long-form narration workflows, and speech-model latency and cost comparisons across Pipecat, LiveKit, Gemini Live, and ElevenLabs.

On November 11, 2026, at its New York Summit, ElevenLabs framed voice and conversational models as the next core infrastructure layer for software—comparable to cloud compute or payments—while unveiling Eleven v4, Scribe v3 Medical, Eleven Realtime, and stateful agent routines [2]. Days earlier, November production pricing audits from engineering shops running OpenAI's gpt-realtime-2.1 and Google's gemini-3.8-live laid bare a sharp gap between demo-dashboard estimates and real call bills [14][15]. Voice agents have clearly moved out of the novelty phase, yet every production stack still collides with an immutable constraint set by human biology: conversational turns flip in roughly 200 milliseconds [1], whereas even a tightly tuned cloud pipeline spends 750 to 950 milliseconds just getting its first syllable back to the listener's ear [5].

Understanding why those extra 600 milliseconds feel so stubborn—and why shaving them down blindly breaks accuracy, safety, and unit economics—requires treating a voice agent as a coupled physical and statistical system rather than a chatbot with a microphone attached.

The 200-Millisecond Floor: Biological Timing Meets Network Physics

In a landmark 2009 cross-linguistic study published in PNAS, Tanya Stivers and colleagues measured conversational turn transitions across ten diverse languages spanning five continents [1]. Across all ten languages, the distribution was unimodal with its peak between 0 and 200 milliseconds after the prior speaker stopped [1]. The overall mean offset across the dataset was +208 milliseconds, stretching across a narrow cultural continuum from +7 milliseconds in Japanese to +469 milliseconds in Danish [1].

Human speakers do not pull off that speed by thinking fast after a sentence ends. Because retrieving words and encoding speech motor plans takes far longer than 200 milliseconds, human listeners run comprehension and response formulation in parallel, predicting syntactic and pragmatic completion before the speaker finishes. Deployed voice assistants miss that cadence by an order of magnitude: Deepgram's August 2026 architecture guide cites a 2025 corpus study of 1,077 Alexa turn transitions that averaged a 1,366-millisecond floor-transfer offset, noting that caller perceived willingness begins to drop past 600 milliseconds and falls sharply between 700 and 800 milliseconds [3].

Before any neural network runs a single matrix multiplication, network physics takes its own bite out of that budget. For real-time telephony planning, the ITU-T G.114 standard treats one-way delay below 150 milliseconds as essentially transparent and 400 milliseconds as an upper ceiling [3]. Traditional carrier paths over the Public Switched Telephone Network (PSTN) or Session Initiation Protocol (SIP) trunks regularly add 200 to 400 milliseconds of transport and transcoding overhead before audio ever hits your inference server [3].

That transport floor explains why WebRTC (Web Real-Time Communication) has become the default transport for interactive browser and mobile agents. Unlike WebSockets running over TCP—where a single dropped packet stalls subsequent audio frames via head-of-line blocking—WebRTC carries media over UDP and builds in hardware-tested Acoustic Echo Cancellation (AEC), noise suppression, and jitter buffering [3][4]. When ElevenLabs migrated its own 11.ai voice assistant from WebSockets to WebRTC in July 2025, it cited client-side echo cancellation and background noise removal as decisive improvements for turn quality, since an agent that hears its own loudspeaker output as user speech will constantly barge in on itself [4].

Anatomy of a Single Turn: Where the 920 Milliseconds Go

To see why framework wars rarely decide real-world speed, trace a single turn through an illustrative, well-tuned WebRTC cascaded pipeline—the classic chain of Voice Activity Detection (VAD), streaming Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), Large Language Model (LLM), and streaming Text-to-Speech (TTS). Evalgent's September 2026 breakdown maps a realistic end-to-end turn measured at the caller's ear at roughly 920 milliseconds [5]:

  1. Turn-detection wait (~300 ms): VAD detects silence, and either a timer or a turn model waits long enough to bet that the speaker has finished rather than merely paused to breathe [5].
  2. STT finalization (~100 ms incremental): The ASR model flushes its final segment after speech stops [5]. Note that in enterprise production traces, total client-side streaming transcript latency (from spoken word to committed text) commonly runs 200 to 500 milliseconds, with Deepgram citing a 2026 enterprise tutorial measuring 337 ms P50 in a self-hosted pipeline and 402 ms P50 with a cloud LLM [3]. Streaming recognition also carries a measurable accuracy tax: a multi-provider benchmark cited in that same guide recorded 10.9% word error rate (WER) in streaming mode versus 9.37% in batch mode [3].
  3. LLM time-to-first-token, or TTFT (~300 ms): The language model ingests the transcript, system prompt, and conversation history and emits its first text token [5]. Any synchronous tool call or database lookup inside this hook explodes the budget immediately [5].
  4. TTS time-to-first-byte, or TTFB (~120 ms): The speech synthesizer converts the opening text clause into the first compressed audio chunk [5].
  5. Transport and playout (~80 ms): Network transit, WebRTC jitter buffer absorption, and device audio hardware playback [5].
  6. Framework overhead (~20 ms): Async event loops, frame routing, and serialization inside Pipecat or LiveKit Agents—roughly 2% of the entire turn [5].

An important caveat keeps engineers from misdiagnosing bottlenecks: you cannot simply add isolated stage numbers in series. In a streaming pipeline, the stages overlap like a relay race where the next runner starts sprinting inside the handoff zone. Streaming ASR transcribes words while the turn-detection clock is already ticking—which is why LiveKit's end_of_utterance_delay metric bundles transcription delay inside the endpointing interval [5]. Likewise, once the LLM streams its first complete phrase or clause, TTS begins synthesizing sentence one while the LLM is still generating sentence two.

Even so, published vendor numbers routinely confuse teams because providers measure different clocks. In its October 29, 2026 latency methodology documentation, ElevenLabs reports median (p50) server-side latency of ~100 ms for Eleven v4 Turbo and Eleven Flash v2.5 (~75 ms raw model inference) and ~165 ms for Eleven v3 Conversational, alongside ~150 ms median time-to-committed-transcript for Scribe v2 Realtime [6][16]. Crucially, ElevenLabs explicitly warns that those numbers exclude network round-trips, upstream LLM wait times, and client playback buffering, and assume a "warm voice" already cached in memory; longer inputs, peak-hour queueing, and Professional Voice Clones push real user-perceived Time-to-First-Audio (TTFA) higher [6].

Independent academic instrumentation confirms how much variance survives in real deployments. In September 2026, Bertil Braun published Voice-Light, an open full-duplex cascaded agent evaluated across three unscripted operator microphone sessions from Germany to European cloud GPUs [7]. Across 36 measured turns (excluding cold starts), the median latency from final VAD endpoint to first server audio was 758 ms, with 21 of 36 turns landing under 800 ms—but the full range stretched from 528 ms to 1,652 ms [7]. When speculative response generation succeeded and was promoted (9 of 13 fully traced turns), median latency dropped to 667 ms; on the four turns where speculation missed and had to restart, median latency jumped to 1,513 ms [7]. The takeaway is clear: median latency numbers tell you how a pipeline behaves when every bet hits; tail latency (p95) tells you what happens when a caller hesitates or a model misses a turn boundary.

The Endpointing Dilemma: Acoustic Clocks vs. Semantic Listeners

If framework plumbing takes only 20 milliseconds, why do two teams running the same LLM and TTS models report wildly different conversational smoothness? Almost all of that variance lives in endpointing—deciding whether a speaker's silence means "I am done, your turn" or "I am thinking, wait." Cut in too early and the agent talks over the caller; wait too long and every pause feels like a walkie-talkie [8].

Classic VAD—models like Silero VAD that classify 20–30 ms audio frames as speech or silence—solves only energy detection, not conversational intent [9]. Raising a static silence timer (stop_secs in Pipecat or min_delay in LiveKit) buys fewer interruptions at the direct cost of dead air [5][8]. Moving to text-only semantic turn detectors—small transformers that read the partial transcript to judge grammatical completion—created a second ceiling. As LiveKit engineers illustrated in June 2026, consider a caller who pauses after saying, "I would like to order one large pizza…" versus "I would like to order one large pizza… and a garlic bread" [8]. At the moment of the pause, the text transcript is word-for-word identical; only prosody—an upward inflection versus a falling terminal pitch—separates an unfinished list from a completed order [8].

By late 2026, the ecosystem has fractured into three distinct engineering philosophies for solving turn detection:

  • Framework-Owned Audio-Semantic Fusion (LiveKit Turn Detector v1.0): Released in June 2026, LiveKit's v1 model replaces transcript-first prediction with dual audio encoders: one projects speech directly into an LLM embedding space for semantic cues without waiting on STT finalization, while a recurrent acoustic branch tracks timing and prosody [8]. On LiveKit's open eot-bench suite across 14 languages, v1 achieved a 9.9% false-cutoff rate at a 300 ms latency budget (versus 12.9% for Deepgram Flux and 27.7% for ultraVAD) and 4.5% at a 600 ms budget (versus 5.5% for Soniox and 9.9% for Flux) [8]. LiveKit argues that keeping turn detection in the orchestration layer decouples conversational feel from STT vendor lock-in [8].
  • Lightweight Edge-CPU Audio Turn Models (Pipecat Smart Turn v3): Released by Daily in September 2025, Smart Turn v3 distills a Whisper Tiny encoder with linear classification layers and static int8 quantization-aware training (QAT) into an 8 MB ONNX model—roughly 50 times smaller than v2—covering 23 languages [9]. Running on CPU with no GPU required, it executes preprocessing plus inference in 12.6 ms on an AWS c7a.2xlarge (59.8 ms on a low-cost c8g.medium) and reaches 94.31% accuracy on English test samples when paired with Silero VAD (stop_secs=0.2 recommended) [5][9].
  • STT-Embedded Turn State Machines (Deepgram Flux): Deepgram takes the opposite architectural stance, embedding conversational turn detection inside the speech recognizer itself on /v2/listen [10]. Rather than emitting dumb pause flags, Flux streams structured TurnInfo events (StartOfTurn, Update, EagerEndOfTurn, TurnResumed, and EndOfTurn), hitting roughly ~260 ms p50 end-of-turn detection at default settings [10]. If EagerEndOfTurn fires prematurely so the LLM can start drafting a reply, a subsequent TurnResumed event tells the orchestrator to cancel the draft before audio plays [10]. Crucially, Deepgram limits Flux to streaming-only use and 10 languages today compared with 45+ languages on Nova-3 [10].

Where vendors claim turn detection is nearly "solved" [8], recent peer-reviewed and preprint research urges sharp skepticism. First, what is established is that fusing acoustic prosody with semantic context beats fixed silence timers on clean benchmarks. What is claimed by single-vendor suites (eot-bench or Smart Turn's test sets) often reflects curated evaluation distributions rather than messy production telephony. In Voice-Light, Braun tested an earlier learned causal turn-completion checkpoint against 1,673 real-conversation silence candidates and found a brutal precision–recall collapse: the learned checkpoint maintained a low 2.70% false-cutoff rate, yet achieved only 12.53% end-of-turn recall, compared with 95.60% recall for a Silero timing policy—forcing the deployed system back onto a hybrid controller [7].

Second, aggressive speculative turn-taking introduces an unexpected safety and correctness failure mode. In LateIntent-Bench (September 2026), Yang Xiao and colleagues tested four native full-duplex speech models across 3,136 sessions where an ambiguous prefix was followed by a controlled pause before revealing either a benign or harmful continuation [11]. Measuring Premature Response Rate (PRR), they found that three of the four models showed elevated harmful engagement when intent arrived late—answering before the disambiguating clause landed—while the fourth lost engagement across both branches [11]. Inserting a 1.5-second silence buffer after intent revelation restored PRR to baseline [11]. Lowering turn latency below 500 milliseconds is not a free lunch; every millisecond shaved off endpointing shifts risk onto intent completeness and cancellation hygiene.

Native Speech-to-Speech vs. Cascaded Stacks: The Intelligence–Dynamics Trade-Off

If coordinating VAD, STT, LLM, and TTS creates compounding delays and brittle turn handoffs, why not collapse the entire pipeline into one end-to-end multimodal Transformer that ingests raw audio and emits raw audio directly? That is the pitch behind native speech-to-speech (S2S) APIs like OpenAI's Realtime API (gpt-realtime-2.1 and gpt-live-1), Google's Gemini Live API (gemini-3.8-live), and ElevenLabs' Eleven Realtime [2][14][15].

Native S2S models do deliver noticeably more fluid full-duplex behavior—handling overlapping barge-ins, paralinguistic tone, and sub-second turnarounds without chaining three separate network hops [14][15]. Yet production teams have been slow to abandon cascaded stacks, and two 2026 papers explain why.

In March 2026, researchers at Sierra.ai and Princeton University introduced τ-Voice, extending τ²-bench into 278 grounded, multi-turn tool-use tasks under both clean and realistic acoustic conditions (background noise, diverse accents, and Gilbert–Elliott packet frame drops) [12]. The degradation was stark: while text-mode GPT-5 (reasoning) achieved 85% pass@1, full-duplex voice agents scored 31–51% on clean audio and fell to 26–38% under realistic audio—retaining just 30% to 45% of text capability [12]. Qualitative error annotation confirmed that 79% to 90% of failures stemmed from agent behavior (policy violations, garbled tool arguments, premature responses, and reasoning slips) rather than unintelligible user audio [12].

A July 2026 reinforcement learning paper, DuplexPO, pinpoints the mechanism behind that collapse [13]. When spoken models are fine-tuned end-to-end on conversational audio to master rapid turn-taking, short backchannels ("mm-hmm", "got it"), and immediate barge-in yielding, their instruction-following and reasoning scores degrade [13]. DuplexPO demonstrates that this "intelligence–dynamics trade-off" is not fundamental: by decoupling when to speak from what to say—optimizing turn initiation, backchanneling, and yielding via a Factorized Conversational Dynamics Reward over short dynamics-critical windows—a spoken model can retain the semantic reasoning of an instruction-tuned LLM while learning sharp temporal control [13].

Until decoupled architectures mature across commercial APIs, cascaded and hybrid "Thinker–Talker" stacks retain major practical advantages for production agents:

  • Inspectable text boundaries: Keeping an explicit transcript between ASR and the LLM lets engineers run deterministic PII redaction, exact keyterm prompting, schema-validated tool calls, and regression evals on text traces—none of which work cleanly inside an opaque audio-in/audio-out checkpoint [3][7].
  • Swappable specialized components: Teams can pair a fast reasoning model with an 8 MB local turn detector and a domain-tuned ASR, or play brief "bridge speech" concurrently while an asynchronous backend tool executes [5][7][9].
  • Authoritative history tracking: As Voice-Light highlights, if an agent generates 400 ms of speculative audio that gets canceled by a user interruption before reaching the speaker's ear, injecting the unsent LLM text into conversation history corrupts multi-turn state; browser playback acknowledgments are required to keep durable memory honest [7].

Provider Economics and Quality: The Hidden Math of Tokens, Caching, and Voices

Latency choices also dictate unit economics, and nowhere are headline numbers more misleading than in late-2026 voice pricing.

Consider a concrete worked cost comparison published in November 2026 by Dograh AI and Fora Soft using an identical 5-minute, 12-turn call (7.5 seconds of caller speech and 15 seconds of agent speech per turn, plus a 2,000-token system prompt with tool definitions) [14][15]. On Google's gemini-3.8-live, sticker rates look negligible: $3.00 per 1M audio input tokens (~$0.005/min) and $12.00 per 1M audio output tokens (~$0.018/min), plus $0.75/1M text input and $4.50/1M text output/thinking [14]. If you naively multiply 1.5 caller minutes by $0.005 and 3 agent minutes by $0.018, you expect $0.0615 [14].

In reality, because the Gemini Live API currently lacks prompt caching and re-bills the accumulated session context on every turn (at ~25 audio tokens per second), the model actually reads 39,375 audio input tokens and 24,000 text tokens across those 12 turns, driving the call bill to $0.1901 (~$0.038/min)—more than three times the raw per-minute meter [14]. Stretch the same call to 10 minutes (24 turns) and audio input balloons to 159,750 tokens, pushing the total to $0.6233 (~$0.062/min): doubling duration more than triples cost, and Extended Thinking tokens add $4.50/1M output on top [14].

OpenAI's gpt-realtime-2.1 illustrates the opposite billing lever: prompt caching. Listed at $32 per 1M audio input tokens and $64 per 1M audio output tokens (with gpt-realtime-2.1-mini at $10/$20), uncached history re-reads send that exact same 5-minute call to $1.1748, and runaway uncached sessions can hit $0.46/min [14][15]. Yet OpenAI discounts cached audio input 80x down to $0.40 per 1M tokens, which pulls real-world budgets with working KV caching down to $0.06–$0.11/min on the flagship and $0.02–$0.05/min on the mini variant [15]. Output audio (~1,200 tokens/min, or $0.077/min spoken on flagship) cannot be cached away, meaning verbose agents stay expensive [15]. For predictable long calls, OpenAI's separate gpt-live-1 endpoint (GA since September 10, 2026) shifts to $0.05 per session minute billed per second plus backend model fees—removing quadratic length scaling, though charging even during silence [15].

Cascaded stacks remain markedly cheaper per minute when teams optimize each layer independently. Deepgram lists both Nova-3 (monolingual streaming) and Flux (English streaming) at $0.0077/min regular pay-as-you-go (with promotional rates dipping to $0.0048 and $0.0065/min) [10]. On the synthesis side, a direct conflict in late-2026 provider claims shows why dates and disclosures matter when reading benchmarks. Cartesia's comparison page states that Sonic 3.6 ranks first on the Artificial Analysis Provider Voice Arena blind-listening test with sub-90 ms first audio across 44 languages [17]. Yet an October 6, 2026 analysis by Haass—which transparently discloses that Haass is an ElevenLabs implementation partner—points out that Cartesia's snapshot compared Sonic against older Eleven v3 and Flash v2.5 models prior to ElevenLabs' September 28, 2026 launch of Eleven v4 [16]. On Artificial Analysis checked in October 2026, Eleven v4 led with 1315 Elo ahead of Cartesia Sonic 3.6 at 1275 and Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS at 1267 [16].

The practical split between TTS providers is therefore architectural rather than absolute. ElevenLabs dominates long-form narration, emotional range, 90+ language coverage, IPA pronunciation control, and enterprise compliance breadth (SOC 2, HIPAA, ISO 27001, PCI DSS, FedRAMP), while offering ~100 ms median server-side latency on Eleven v4 Turbo and Flash v2.5 [6][16]. Cartesia remains the sharper fit for English-first high-volume utility voice agents optimizing raw cost per minute—and for organizations requiring mature on-premises or air-gapped deployments in production today, where ElevenLabs' on-prem offerings were still in early access as of October 2026 [16].

What to Watch Next

Three concrete shifts over the next two quarters will decide whether voice agents finally close the gap between 750 ms demos and effortless 300 ms conversations:

  • Mandatory Early-2027 API Migrations: Teams cannot treat late-2026 model choices as static. OpenAI shuts down legacy gpt-realtime and gpt-realtime-mini on January 20, 2027, pushing workloads to gpt-realtime-2.1 (where flagship text output rises from $16 to $24 per 1M tokens) [15], while ElevenLabs has scheduled deprecations for legacy audio models in January and March 2027 [6]. Re-benchmarking prompt-cache hit rates and TTFA under the replacement endpoints will be mandatory.
  • Decoupled "Thinker–Talker" Runtimes: The convergence of DuplexPO-style temporal RL policies [13], 8–12 ms local acoustic turn detectors [8][9], and asynchronous tool execution (with audible bridge speech) points toward hybrid dual-speed agents: a tiny, always-on acoustic controller managing sub-200 ms yielding and backchannels while a full reasoning LLM handles grounded tool calls without losing half its intelligence [7][12].
  • End-to-End Audio-Native Evaluation: Isolated TTS median latency claims are losing credibility. With open suites like LiveKit's eot-bench [8], Sierra and Princeton's τ-Voice [12], and LateIntent-Bench [11], engineering teams can finally evaluate turn-taking false-cutoff curves, p95 end-to-end latency, delayed-intent safety, and grounded task completion together on noisy, accented 8 kHz phone audio before putting an agent in front of callers.
