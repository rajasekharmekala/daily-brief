Cosmos and Life

Webb has three black holes feeding in one galaxy and a lava planet with reflective clouds, Swift ends its science mission and a Cambridge simulation questions comets as the source of life's building blocks.

Space, astronomy and cosmology

Nature: 2 days ago · 5m
NASA's Swift observatory ends science operations and is expected to re-enter around 2 November  —  After a failed rescue attempt, NASA has shut off science operations on the 22-year-old Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, which had drifted too low to lock onto targets. Some researchers expect it to re-enter the atmosphere on 2 November, with most of it burning up. Swift identified almost 2,000 gamma-ray bursts and helped show that neutron-star collisions make heavy elements, and its archive remains open to researchers.
Takeaway: Follows the end of a major high-energy and gravitational-wave counterpart observatory, relevant to your LIGO and black-hole interest.
ScienceDaily (Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics release): 4 days ago · 4m
Webb finds three actively feeding supermassive black holes in one galaxy 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang  —  Using Webb's NIRSpec integral field mode, astronomers found three accreting black holes in galaxy J0148-4214 at redshift z=5.02. Two lie near the centre, only 620 light-years apart (projected) and expected to merge; the third sits about 5,500 light-years out. The authors call it the first evidence of three active black holes in one distant galaxy, with implications for mergers that future gravitational-wave observatories may detect.
Takeaway: Ties JWST early-universe results to black-hole mergers and future gravitational-wave detection, both on your space list.
University of Waterloo News: 3 days ago · 4m
Webb phase curve points to reflective silicate clouds on lava planet TOI-561 b  —  A 37-hour Webb observation of the lava world TOI-561 b, covering nearly four orbits, shows the planet reflects roughly three-quarters of the starlight that hits it, comparable to Venus, and redistributes heat to its night side. Climate models suggest silicate clouds on the dayside could explain this and help it keep an atmosphere. What the atmosphere is made of, such as carbon dioxide or sulfur dioxide, is still open. Published in The Astronomical Journal.
Takeaway: A concrete JWST exoplanet-atmosphere result, in line with your exoplanets interest.
NASA: 3 days ago · 5m
Perseverance measured ultrafine Martian dust on its spacesuit-material samples  —  Over 1,645 Martian days, Perseverance's PIXL instrument detected dust particles smaller than any measured on Mars before: fine particles under 2.5 micrometers and ultrafine ones around 100 nanometers, on calibration targets and spacesuit swatches. Martian grains are rounder, so less abrasive than lunar dust, and are enriched in sulfur, chlorine and iron. Findings were due in PNAS on 27 October and could inform suit and habitat design.
Takeaway: A Mars-mission result from your list that ties rover data to future crewed exploration.
American Meteor Society: 6 days ago · 3m
Meteor outlook: Taurids to peak in early November with a darkening Moon  —  The weekly outlook says the full Moon on 26 October washes out most meteors, but dark windows between dusk and moonrise grow each night, a good time for slow Taurids. Its table lists the Southern Taurids peaking on 5 November and the Northern Taurids on 12 November, each at about 1 meteor per hour from mid-northern latitudes. Expect a few bright fireballs rather than a storm.
Takeaway: A practical sky-events item for evenings around San Jose over the next two weeks.

Biology and science

Phys.org (University of Cambridge release; Scientific Reports): yesterday · 4m
Comets probably did not seed early Earth with life's building blocks, Cambridge simulations suggest  —  A Cambridge-led team modelled comet impacts, comet-atmosphere interaction and comet dynamics, focusing on hydrogen cyanide, a key prebiotic molecule. Comets arrive too fast and at too steep an angle for the fragile organics to survive, and impacts delivering useful concentrations would have been extremely rare, at best a 'one-off fluke'. Asteroid impacts or chemistry on Earth itself remain candidates. Mars fares slightly better, though still unlikely.
Takeaway: Fits your origin-of-life interest: it weighs whether comets or Earth-based chemistry supplied life's early building blocks.
Nature: Oct 21 · 8m
Protein analysis assigns Xujiayao fossils to Denisovans and sketches their skull shape  —  Researchers analysed a skull and a jaw fragment from Xujiayao in northern China (about 199 to 138 thousand years old). Proteomics recovered 18 endogenous proteins, including a collagen variant, COL1A2-996 R>K, specific to Denisovans. Combined with 3D shape comparisons, this places Xujiayao and several other East Asian fossils in one Denisovan lineage: low skull, massive brow ridge, flat mid-face, weak jutting of the jaw.
Takeaway: Matches your human-origins and archaeology interest: ancient protein, not DNA, is now putting a face on a group known mostly from genomes.
Quanta Magazine: yesterday · 10m
How spiders turn 'silk dope' into one of nature's toughest materials  —  Quanta explains new work led by Gregory Holland at San Diego State University showing that the liquid protein mix in a spider's gland first reorganises into semiliquid droplets called condensates before hardening into fibre. The piece traces the history of competing theories (liquid crystal, micelles) and why condensates, now seen across cell biology, give a practical guide for copying silk in the lab.
Takeaway: Broad biology interest: a clear explainer on protein phase separation, a mechanism that recurs across cell biology and evolution.
Nature Communications: Oct 9 · 10m
Rearranged 3D genome structure may underlie the evolution of cephalopod nervous systems  —  A Vienna-led team combined Micro-C, RNA-seq and ATAC-seq across squid, cuttlefish and octopus. A large ancient chromosomal rearrangement in their common ancestor brought distant genome regions together; chromatin domains stayed broadly conserved while hundreds of loops were species-specific. A CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of one regulatory sequence showed these loops matter for neural development. The authors propose genome topology helps maintain complex traits.
Takeaway: Touches your evolution and genetics interests: a case where genome folding, not new genes, is linked to the rise of complex brains.
Quanta Magazine: 6 days ago · 10m
Malaria parasite caught forming its temporary 'door' into red blood cells  —  Plasmodium enters red blood cells through a ring called the moving junction, a gap that opens and closes too fast and too small for earlier imaging to resolve. Chi-Min Ho's lab at Columbia University imaged it at the moment of invasion and found the parasite installs molecular gaskets and remodels the host membrane to slip through without a trace. The work could inform malaria drug design.
Takeaway: Broad interest in cell biology and structural imaging: cryo-electron methods resolving a process that was invisible until now.
