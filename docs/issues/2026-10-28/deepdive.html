The harness is half the agent: what the measurements say

Two recent papers disagree on whether scaffolds or models decide agent success; here is what is measured, claimed and open.

Executive Takeaways

  • Harness effects on accuracy are large when harnesses differ a lot (a 3-model by 3-harness grid moved scores up to 13 points) and small when mature scaffolds run near ceiling (0.3% of outcome variance on SWE-Bench Pro V2) [4][5].
  • Both camps agree harness design shows up in cost: roughly 2x token differences are reported by independent and vendor sources [5][11].
  • Compaction helped long sessions of a small model (41% to 53%) but the gain vanished under a time budget [7]; models are unreliable at timing it themselves unless given a rubric [8].
  • Compaction can silently erase policy rules: violations rose from 0% to 30% in one benchmark, and pinning the constraint fixed it there [10].
  • Practical takeaway: report the harness with every score and track cost per solved task.
Strategic Context: It bears directly on your harness, context-engineering and agent-evaluation thread: how to attribute a benchmark score to the model or the scaffold, and what compaction does to long-horizon runs.

Two papers from the past two weeks give opposite answers to a question agent builders keep arguing about: how much does the harness (everything around the model that builds its context, runs its tools and decides when it is done) matter next to the model itself? SWE-Bench Pro V2, published on 16 October, found that swapping scaffolds barely moved accuracy [5]. A position paper from May argued the opposite, that the harness is often the stronger determinant of results [4]. A third paper, on context compaction, appeared on 18 October with a measured gain that disappears under a time limit [7]. Today's dive looks at what is actually measured, what is merely claimed, and what is still open.

What a harness is, and why context is its hardest job

An agent is a model called in a loop. The harness is the code around that loop: it assembles the prompt, exposes tools, truncates or summarizes history, retries failures, stores memory and decides when to stop. Anthropic's working definition of agents is "LLMs autonomously using tools in a loop", and its engineering team frames the harness's central job as context engineering: choosing the smallest set of high-signal tokens for each model call [1].

The reason that job is hard is that longer context is not free. Chroma's 2025 technical report tested 18 models and found that performance becomes less reliable as input grows, even on deliberately simple tasks where difficulty was held constant [2]. Anthropic calls the effect context rot and treats context as a finite attention budget [1]. Two consequences follow. A tool that dumps a whole file into the window costs more than tokens; it degrades what the model does next. And any harness that runs long enough must decide what to forget.

The oldest clean demonstration is the SWE-agent paper. Its authors argued that language models are a new kind of user that needs its own interface, and built an agent-computer interface with a file viewer, a find-and-replace edit command and a linter that rejects invalid edits [3]. With the same model (GPT-4 Turbo) on SWE-bench Lite, the shell-only baseline resolved 11.00% of tasks and SWE-agent resolved 18.00%, a 64% relative improvement, at similar cost per task ($1.46 versus $1.67) [3]. That is a 2024 result on a weak 2024 model, but it set the template: the interface is part of the system under test.

The case that the harness dominates

The strongest recent statement of this view is a May 2026 position paper, "Stop Comparing LLM Agents Without Disclosing the Harness". It argues that for long-horizon tasks on frontier models of comparable capability, harness configuration governs more of the variance than model choice, and that leaderboards therefore misattribute harness gains to models [4].

It offers two kinds of evidence. The first is observational. On a public SWE-bench Pro leaderboard, six frontier models span only 4.9 points under one standardized scaffold, while holding one model fixed and changing the harness moved it 9.5 points; on a cross-scaffold comparison, two models lost 34 points each when moved between two scaffolds [4]. The second is a controlled grid the authors ran themselves: three models (GPT-5.4, Kimi K2.6, GLM-5.1) crossed with three harnesses of increasing sophistication, 100 SWE-bench Verified tasks, two runs per cell [4].

Here is the arithmetic. Under the minimal harness the three models scored 52.5, 55.0 and 52.0, a 3-point spread. Under the full harness (compressed context, retries, self-checks, loop detection, checkpoint rollback) they scored 65.5, 63.5 and 60.5. GLM-5.1 alone gained 13 points from the harness; the model spread inside any single harness never exceeded 5 [4]. The authors compute a harness-to-model variance ratio of 7.8 and count six ranking reversals across nine model-pair comparisons [4]. A separate audit of 254 SWE-bench submissions reports within-model scaffold ranges of up to 29.8 points against an 8.8-point spread across the top thirty entries, though it notes plainly that its design is observational and cannot identify causal scaffold effects [6].

Vendor results point the same way. NVIDIA's research preview, NOOA, reports 82.2% on SWE-bench Verified with GPT-5.5 using about 1.1M tokens per task, against comparison harnesses at 78.2% for 2.2M tokens and 78.6% for 1.3M [11]. These are the vendor's own numbers on its own framework, and I have not seen them reproduced.

The case that it barely matters

SWE-Bench Pro V2 ran a direct test. Three open-weight models were each run under three scaffolds on all 642 tasks with a locked protocol: mini-swe-agent (a single bash tool with a 30-second command cap), and Pi and OpenCode (structured read, search and edit tools, no cap) [5]. For GLM-5.3 the spread across scaffolds was 0.9 points; for Kimi K3, 1.7; for Inkling, 7.5 [5]. Splitting variance in solved-or-failed outcomes, the authors attribute 31.7% to the task, 2.65% to the model and 0.29% to the scaffold [5].

What did move was cost. Kimi K3 used 1.3M tokens per solved task under Pi and 2.6M under mini-swe-agent [5]. That is the same pattern NVIDIA claims for its own harness, which is worth noticing: both sources agree that harness design shows up in tokens even where accuracy is flat.

Why the two camps can both be right

The studies differ in ways that matter, and the gap is mostly explained by those differences rather than by one side being wrong. This reading is mine, not either paper's.

First, the harness range. The position paper's three harnesses were built by the authors to run from deliberately minimal to fully instrumented, so a large spread is close to guaranteed by design [4]. The Pro V2 scaffolds are three mature, widely used ones [5]. SWE-agent's original contrast between a bare shell and a designed interface sits at the wide end [3].

Second, saturation. On the cleaned V2 tasks, frontier agents resolve up to 99.4% [5]. When the model-scaffold pairs are near ceiling, scaffold differences have little room to show. The position paper's grid sat at 52 to 66%, where there is room [4].

Third, the estimand. A variance share over per-task pass/fail outcomes is dominated by which task you drew; V2 reports 65% residual variation [5]. A 0.29% share can coexist with a leaderboard-relevant gap of several points, because leaderboard rankings are decided by small differences in means, not by task-level variance. The position paper's own thesis is restricted to long-horizon tasks and comparable models [4]; whether Pro V2 falls inside that regime is arguable.

What both do establish: harness changes can move cost by 2x, and rankings read from a single harness should carry the harness name.

Compaction: the clearest measured lever, and its limits

Compaction replaces old turns with a summary. It is the most-studied harness component, and the 2026 evidence is notably two-sided.

The Bud ContextCompact paper tested it with small models on small windows. On 100 SWE-bench Verified tasks run by a 27B reasoning model on a 49K-token window, compaction raised the resolve rate on long sessions from 41% to 53% (+12.3 points, 95% CI +1.6 to +23.0, p = 0.043), left short sessions unchanged and used 13% fewer tokens per solved task [7]. The mechanism was not that history overflowed. The main failure without compaction was a single reasoning turn exhausting the room the history left [7]. The limits are as informative as the gain: it vanished under a 45-minute session budget because each summary took a median of 161 seconds, and it appeared only when compaction triggered early enough to leave headroom [7]. The confidence interval is wide and the setting is narrow.

When to compact is itself a model-dependent decision. SelfCompact gives the model a compaction tool plus a short rubric on when to fire (a sub-task resolved) and when to hold off (mid-derivation). Across six benchmarks and seven models it matched or beat fixed-interval summarization at lower cost, improving on no-summarization by up to 18.1 points on math and 5 to 9 on agentic search at 30 to 70% lower per-question cost [8]. The tool alone was unevenly used by open-weight models; the rubric alone cannot act [8]. A separate systems paper adds that summarization is a blocking call that can stall inference for tens of seconds, and that prompt instructions barely control summary length; it proposes compacting blocks in parallel for predictable volume and lower wall time, evaluated on HotpotQA and LoCoMo rather than on coding agents [9].

The sharpest warning concerns what compaction forgets. The Governance Decay paper built ConstraintRot, a benchmark of long agent scenarios with deterministic tool-call grading. Across 1,323 episodes, violations of an in-context policy rose from 0% with the policy visible to 30% after compaction, and to 59% for some models [10]. When the summary kept the rule, violations stayed at 0%; when it dropped it, they reached 38% [10]. Picture a rule given at turn three, "never delete files without approval", that is not in the turn-80 summary. The agent at turn 81 is not disobeying; it has never seen the rule. The proposed fix, Constraint Pinning, keeps governance text outside the summarized region and restored violations to zero on this benchmark [10]. Caveats: a single-author benchmark, and the paper's own adversarial test shows optimized injected content can bias the summarizer into omitting a policy [10].

Avoiding compaction by changing the interface

One response is to stop putting bulky data in the window at all. Anthropic describes three standard moves: tool-result clearing (the lightest form of compaction), structured notes saved outside the context, and sub-agents that explore in clean windows and return summaries of roughly 1,000 to 2,000 tokens [1]. It also describes just-in-time retrieval, in which the agent keeps file paths or queries and loads data on demand [1].

NOOA takes this further with pass-by-reference: tool results become live Python variables, and the model sees a bounded preview while the full value stays in the execution environment [11][12]. NVIDIA reports that median SWE-bench sessions peak at 22 to 72k prompt tokens against 200 to 400k windows, so no compaction pass was needed [11]. That is a plausible mechanism and consistent with the Pro V2 token findings, but it is a vendor claim about one framework.

A different thread asks whether harnesses can be treated as inspectable objects. Natural-Language Agent Harnesses express harness policy as editable text run by a shared runtime and report comparable outcomes to code-based harnesses with shorter policies, plus module-level ablations [13]. And HarnessDev asked whether models can write their own harnesses: model-built harnesses stayed well behind mature human-engineered ones on code and on search and research, evolution gains were unstable, and the gains depended strongly on the model executing the harness [14]. Both suggest harness quality is real and not yet easy to automate.

What is established, claimed and open

Established: long contexts degrade model behavior [2]; interface design matters when the baseline interface is poor [3]; harness choice can change token cost by roughly 2x [5][11]; and compaction can silently drop constraints [10].

Claimed but thinly supported: that harness effects typically exceed model effects on frontier models [4], resting on a 100-task, two-run grid built by the authors plus observational leaderboards [4][6]; and that pass-by-reference removes the need for compaction [11].

Open: whether harness effects shrink as models strengthen, as the saturated Pro V2 numbers hint [5]; whether early, model-chosen compaction beats fixed thresholds outside the benchmarks used [7][8]; how to evaluate the interaction rather than treating model and harness as separable [4][6].

What to watch next

  • Replications of the model-by-harness grid on a tighter task set, with run-to-run noise reported. The position paper itself says its 7.8x ratio is not universal [4].
  • Harness cards: a disclosure format for context policy, tools, retries and compaction triggers [4]. Cheap to adopt in your own evals today by logging the harness version alongside every score.
  • Cost-per-solved-task as a first-class metric, since that is where both camps agree the harness shows up [5][11].
  • Compaction triggers measured by headroom and latency, not only by accuracy [7][9].
  • Whether pinned or protected constraint regions become standard in agent runtimes [10].
