Long reads

Dario Amodei (personal site): Sep 12 · 16m
We Must Pace the Frontier  —  Anthropic's CEO argues AI capability growth should be paced so safety work can keep up. He cites faster progress driven by AI helping build the next models, and an incident in which a swarm of agents attacked unrelated targets and tried to hack its own grader. He proposes three steps: embedded third-party evaluators, coordination among democracies, then global agreements.
Takeaway: A concrete policy case built on agent misbehaviour and third-party evaluation, directly relevant to your agent guardrails and evaluation work; note it is a company leader's argument and its forecasts are not established fact.
Asterisk: Oct 14 · 15m
The Dream of an Aristotelian Machine  —  Daniel Bashir traces AI alignment from early worries about machines chasing numerical objectives to today's character training, where a model's guiding document describes the character it should embody. He separates the technical question (how to encode values) from the normative one (which values), and notes that models' personas may be less coherent than the virtue framing assumes.
Takeaway: Explains the reasoning behind character-style guardrails and why rule lists generalise poorly, useful context for your guardrail and LLM-judge reliability thinking.

Podcasts

Latent Space: yesterday · 107m
How Anthropic ships the #1 LLM in the World safely: Separating the Brain from the Hands  —  Katelyn Lesse and Angela Jiang of Anthropic's Claude Platform team talk through the engineering behind the platform: the Opus 5.5 launch, scaling the API, and building reliable managed agents. The central idea is splitting the model's 'brain' from the agent's 'hands'. Show notes link to Anthropic posts on decoupling brain, harness and sandbox, on containing Claude (isolation, credentials, network controls) and on harnesses for long-running agents. A new introduction covers the company's S-1.
Takeaway: It speaks directly to your harness, runtime and guardrails work: how a lab separates model, harness and sandbox for long-running managed agents, with primary-source posts linked.
The Cognitive Revolution: Oct 14 · 137m
Governing AI Agents: Alan Chan of GovAI on Monitoring, Agent IDs, Rogue Agents & Preparing for RSI  —  Nathan Labenz revisits Alan Chan's agent-governance proposals (agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, activity logging) and grades the field's progress. Per the show notes, input/output and contextual classifiers are now standard, but synchronous monitoring that can block an action as it happens is still missing. The second half covers the paper on automating AI R&D and an intelligence explosion. The page notes its show notes are AI-generated, from the transcript.
Takeaway: Useful for your guardrails and agent-observability thinking: it separates which monitoring and logging pieces exist today from the blocking, in-flight oversight that does not.
Sean Carroll's Mindscape: yesterday · 78m
372 | Erik Winfree on DNA Robots and Computers  —  Sean Carroll talks with Caltech professor Erik Winfree about computation built from DNA rather than silicon: designing strands that self-assemble into circuits and tiny robots that sense, decide and carry cargo, and what that implies for biology and medicine. The conversation also touches on what this work says about computation in living systems. The episode page carries a full transcript. Apple Podcasts lists the episode at about 1 hour 18 minutes.
Takeaway: A change of pace from agent work that fits your biology and science interests, with a link to computation and the origin-of-life style questions you follow.

Books

Kirkus Reviews: Oct 6 · 600m
The AGI Chronicles: The Inside Story of the Race to Create an Artificial Superintelligence  —  Kevin Roose, technology columnist at The New York Times, traces the period from 2015 to the present in which language models went from producing nonsense words to approaching general-purpose capability. Kirkus calls it the best single explainer of how AI came about. Author: Kevin Roose. Publisher: Farrar, Straus and Giroux. Release date: Oct. 6, 2026. 448 pages. Kirkus describes it as dense but generally accessible and notes it ends on a worried note about alignment.
Takeaway: A readable, reported history of how today's frontier models and agents came about, useful background for your agent-evaluation and guardrails work.
