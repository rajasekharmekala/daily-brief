Palakura Kodiguddu Vepudu (Spinach Egg Fry)పాలకూర కోడిగుడ్డు వేపుడు

The everyday Andhra spinach and egg fry, built the way home cooks do it: a cumin-garlic ghee base, dry-fried spinach so nothing turns watery, then eggs set on top and broken into the greens. Garlicky, peppery and properly hot, ready in 18 minutes.

  • 17 g fat
  • 247 kcal
  • 18 min

Ingredients

  • curry leaves1 sprig · 2 g
  • garlic, crushed2 small cloves · 5 g
  • onion, very finely chopped2 tbsp · 30 g
  • green chillies, slit2 chillies · 8 g
  • turmeric1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • Guntur red chilli powder1.5 tsp · 3 g
  • coriander (dhaniya) powder3/4 tsp · 1.5 g
  • garam masala1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • black pepper, freshly ground1/4 tsp · 0.5 g
  • salt, added at the end1/2 tsp · 3 g
Method
  1. Prep (7 min): wash the spinach, drain it well and pat it dry with a towel; chop finely. Wet spinach is what makes this dish soggy and eggy-smelling, so take the extra minute.
  2. Heat the ghee in a wide kadai over medium heat. Add the cumin and let it sizzle for 10 seconds, then the curry leaves; when they stop spluttering add the garlic and green chillies and fry 20 seconds until the garlic is pale gold, not brown.
  3. Add the onion and fry 2 to 3 minutes until translucent with a few golden edges. Stir in the turmeric, chilli powder and coriander powder and cook 15 seconds only, so the karam does not burn.
  4. Add the spinach and toss on medium-high heat for 3 to 4 minutes, uncovered, until it wilts and the water has cooked off and the oil starts to show at the edges.
  5. Push the spinach to the sides, lower the heat, and crack the eggs into the gaps (or break them in and lightly break the yolks). Cover and cook on low for 2 minutes without stirring, until the whites are set.
  6. Flip the set pieces with a spatula, cover 1 more minute, then break everything up and fold it together for 1 to 2 minutes until the eggs are fully cooked and the mix is moist but not wet.
  7. Turn off the heat, then add the salt, garam masala and pepper and mix gently. Rest 1 minute and eat hot.
Make it taste like home
  • Salt goes in after the eggs are done: early salt pulls water from the spinach and the eggs turn rubbery and smell eggy.
  • Keto tip: the carbs here are almost all from the onion and spinach (about 5 g net per serving), so keep the onion to 2 tablespoons; garlic and chillies give the punch instead.
  • Heat: drop to 1 green chilli and 2 g (1 tsp) chilli powder for heat 2; add extra pepper for heat 5.
  • Make-ahead: wilt and dry-fry the spinach masala the night before; reheat in the pan and add the eggs fresh.
Swaps
  • Replace ghee with groundnut oil for a more traditional flavour.
  • Use mustard seeds in the tempering (1/2 tsp) if you like the classic Andhra talimpu.

Royyala Iguru (Andhra Prawn Dry Curry)రొయ్యల ఇగురు

Plump prawns tossed over caramelised onions, curry leaves and green chilli with garam masala and a squeeze of lemon. A 22-minute coastal Andhra classic, dry enough to eat with a spoon or over cauliflower rice.

  • ginger, crushed (half for the marinade, half for the pan)2 cm piece · 6 g

  • ghee2 tsp · 10 g

  • coriander (dhaniya) powder1 tsp · 2 g
  • garam masala1 tsp · 2 g
  • coriander leaves, chopped1 tbsp · 5 g
Method
  1. Prep (8 min): pat the prawns completely dry, then toss with half the crushed ginger-garlic, 1 tsp of the chilli powder, the turmeric, half the salt and 1 tsp of the lemon juice. Leave them on the counter while you chop and start the pan; do not marinate longer than 10 minutes.
  2. Heat the ghee and oil in a wide pan over medium-high heat. Add the cumin, curry leaves and green chillies and cook 20 to 30 seconds until the curry leaves stop spluttering and turn crisp.
  3. Add the onion with a pinch of salt and fry about 5 minutes, stirring, until deep golden brown at the edges. Add the rest of the ginger-garlic and stir 30 seconds so it does not burn.
  4. Raise the heat to high, add the prawns in a single layer and cook 2 minutes, turning once, just until they start to turn pink.
  5. Add the remaining chilli powder, the coriander powder and the garam masala, stir to coat 30 seconds, then sprinkle over 3 tbsp water, cover and cook on medium 3 minutes. The prawns are done when they curl into a loose C; a tight O means overcooked.
  6. Uncover and cook on high 1 to 2 minutes, tossing, until the water has evaporated and the masala clings to the prawns. Finish with the remaining lemon juice and the coriander. Serve straight away.
Make it taste like home
  • Taste tip: the iguru gets its depth from properly browned onion; resist adding tomato or coconut, which would make it a different dish and add carbs.
  • Keto tip: 70 g of onion across two servings keeps each portion to about 6.5 g net carbs. Use lemon, not tamarind, for sourness.
  • Prawns that sit in the pan after cooking turn rubbery, so serve immediately. Frozen shrimp from US stores (peeled, deveined, tail-off) work well; thaw in the fridge overnight and pat dry.
  • Heat dial: for heat 3, use 1 tsp chilli powder and one green chilli.
Swaps
  • Large shrimp work as well; cook an extra minute.
  • Add 100 g chopped gongura at step 5 for a sour royyala gongura.

Method
  1. Prep (8 min): pick the gongura leaves off the stems, wash and chop them roughly, then spin or pat them dry so they do not add extra water. Chop the onion very fine, mince the ginger and garlic and slit the chillies. Drain the mince if it looks wet.
  2. Heat the ghee and sesame oil in a wide kadai over medium-high heat. Add the cumin, dried chillies and curry leaves for 15 seconds, until the curry leaves stop spluttering.
  3. Add the onion with a pinch of salt and fry about 4 minutes until golden brown. Add the ginger, garlic and green chillies and fry 1 minute.
  4. Turn the heat to high, add the goat keema and fry, breaking it up, 4 minutes until there is no pink left. Add the chilli powder, turmeric, coriander powder and the remaining salt and stir 1 minute.
  5. Add the gongura, mix well and cook 2 minutes until it collapses into a dark green paste, then pour in 60 ml (4 tbsp) water, cover and cook on medium-low 7 minutes until the mince is tender and the leaves have melted into the masala.
  6. Uncover and fry on medium-high 3 minutes, stirring often, until the water is gone and oil glistens around the edges. Stir in the garam masala, rest 2 minutes and serve with cauliflower rice or an omelette.
Make it taste like home

  • Where to buy: Indian grocers sell fresh gongura bunches in summer and frozen chopped gongura year-round, and US online Indian grocers list fresh bunches too. Thaw frozen leaves and squeeze out the water before using.
  • Keto tip: 100 g of gongura adds about 4 g net carbs for the whole pot (Indian Food Composition Tables value for green leaves), so this lands at about 8 g net carbs per serving; skip store-bought gongura pickle here, since commercial pickles are very salty and oily.
  • Heat dial: for heat 3, halve the chilli powder and use only one green chilli.
Swaps
  • Use fresh spinach plus 2 tsp lemon juice if you cannot find gongura (a flavour compromise, not authentic).
  • Replace the goat with ground lamb.

The keto stand-in for annam: cauliflower grains wrung dry, then tossed through a sizzling ghee talimpu of mustard, curry leaves, garlic and chilli and lifted with lemon. It smells like lemon rice and soaks up pulusu and kura just as happily.

  • 3 g protein
  • 8 g fat
  • 105 kcal
  • 20 min
  • Serves 3
Fat 66%Protein 13%Net carbs 22%per serving
Ingredients
  • cauliflower florets (about 1 small head)about 4 cups of florets · 400 g
  • ghee1½ tbsp · 20 g
  • mustard seeds1 tsp · 2 g
  • dried red chillies, snipped2 whole · 3 g
  • green chillies, slit1 to 2 · 10 g
  • garlic, crushed2 cloves · 4 g
  • curry leavesabout 20 leaves · 4 g
  • turmeric powder¼ tsp · 1 g
  • lemon juice2 tsp · 12 g
  • salt¾ tsp · 3 g
Method
  1. Pulse the florets in a food processor in short bursts, 6 to 8 times, until the pieces look like rice grains. Stop before they turn to paste. With no processor, grate the florets on the coarse side of a box grater.
  2. Tip the grains into a clean thin kitchen towel, twist it shut and squeeze hard over the sink until almost no water comes out. Fluff the dry grains with a fork. This step is what stops the dish steaming into porridge.
  3. Heat the ghee in a wide kadai on medium-high until it shimmers. Add the mustard seeds and wait about 20 seconds for them to crackle, then drop the heat to medium.
  4. Add the red chillies, curry leaves, garlic and green chillies. Stand back, because the leaves spit. Fry 30 to 40 seconds until the curry leaves stop spluttering and look crisp and glassy and the garlic turns pale gold.
  5. Stir in the turmeric and salt for 5 seconds, then tip in the cauliflower. Raise the heat to high and toss for 4 to 5 minutes, uncovered, until the grains turn from chalky white to slightly translucent with a few golden specks but still have a bite.
  6. Switch off the heat, add the lemon juice, toss and taste for salt. Rest 2 minutes with the lid off so the steam escapes, then serve hot.
Make it taste like home
  • The talimpu does the talking: get the ghee properly hot so the mustard crackles and the curry leaves go crisp, then add the lemon only off the heat so it stays bright.
  • Keto note: one serving is about 130 g of raw cauliflower. The classic lemon-rice tempering of urad dal, chana dal, peanuts or cashews is left out on purpose; for crunch use 1 tbsp toasted sesame seeds instead (roughly 1 g net carb).
  • Make ahead: cooked cauliflower rice keeps about 4 days in the fridge and freezes well. Reheat in a hot dry skillet for 2 to 3 minutes rather than the microwave so it stays fluffy.
  • Serve under any pulusu, gongura mamsam or egg curry, with a spoonful of kobbari karam and a teaspoon of ghee on top.
Swaps
  • Lower heat: skip the green chilli and use 1 dried red chilli.
  • Frozen riced cauliflower works: thaw it fully and squeeze it very dry before it goes in the pan.
  • Dairy-free: use sesame oil instead of ghee.

Ingredient Dossier

Guntur Sannam Chillies and Sesame (Nuvvula) Oil in Andhra Keto Cooking

In coastal Andhra kitchens, true heat and depth do not come from sugar-laden restaurant pastes or commercial thickeners; they are built through dry spices bloomed in smoking-hot fat. Guntur Sannam (Capsicum annuum var. Sannam S4) is the benchmark chilli of the Palnadu and Krishna delta: slender, thick-skinned, intensely red, and measuring between 35,000 and 45,000 Scoville Heat Units. Crucially for a ketogenic diet, Guntur chilli powder carries a high capsanthin pigment content and pungent capsaicinoids that deliver fiery heat without requiring bulk onion or tomato pastes that would otherwise drive up carbohydrate counts.

When tempered in cold-pressed sesame oil (nuvvula nune / gingelly oil) or farm ghee, the fat-soluble capsaicin distributes evenly across the pan, creating a fragrant talimpu that seals meats and greens rapidly. In Andhra-style vepudu and iguru, blooming a teaspoon of freshly ground Guntur karam alongside bruised garlic and spluttering curry leaves creates an emulsion that coats prawns or keema with virtually zero added carbohydrates (under 0.8g net carbs per teaspoon of pure chilli powder). Pairing this with pressed sesame oil provides heart-healthy monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats, sesamol antioxidants, and an earthy, nutty base that grounds the chilli heat.

Automating the Prompt: How Textual Gradients, DSPy MIPROv2, and GEPA Search the Space of Agent Prompts

From natural-language gradients to trace reflection: how automated optimizers mutate agent instructions, where they beat humans, and where they overfit.

Executive Analytical Takeaways

  • Discrete prompt optimization has evolved from trial-and-error to systematic search: TextGrad backpropagates rich natural-language critiques as textual gradients through execution graphs [1].
  • Multi-stage frameworks like DSPy MIPROv2 coordinate Bayesian optimization over demonstration subsets with LLM instruction mutation, outperforming hand-crafted prompts on complex benchmarks [2].
  • Trajectory-aware optimizers (GEPA and VeriEvolve) parse tool-call traces, precondition errors, and intermediate states to diagnose failure modes before modifying system instructions [7].
  • Automated search easily overfits to evaluation rubrics; without held-out test sets and length penalties, optimizers reward-hack verbosity and benchmark-specific heuristics [8].
Strategic Relevance

Directly connects to your daily focus on agent evaluation, trace observability, and building benchmark harnesses for prompt-optimization loops.

For years, building an effective LLM application began and ended with hand-tuning system prompts. An engineer would draft instructions, run twenty queries, observe a formatting glitch or a tool-calling mistake, append a defensive paragraph ("Always output valid JSON; do NOT invent parameters"), and redeploy. In a single-prompt zero-shot chatbot, this ad-hoc craftsmanship could achieve passable results. But in compound AI systems—where multiple agents collaborate, execute tools, query vector indexes, and parse unstructured environment returns—manual prompt engineering rapidly hits a brick wall.

A prompt change that fixes tool selection in step two frequently breaks query formatting in step four. Furthermore, frontier language models behave non-linearly: adding a single phrase like "Think step by step" can improve GSM8K math accuracy by 15% while degrading instruction following on formatting tasks by 8%. Manual tweaking across a twelve-step agentic graph is not engineering; it is stochastic whack-a-mole.

The shift underway in 2025 and 2026 treats prompts not as sacred human prose, but as continuous or discrete parameters within an optimization graph. Instead of human intuition guessing which wording minimizes downstream errors, automated prompt optimizers compile, mutate, and backpropagate error signals through compound workflows. From natural language gradients in OPRO [4] and TextGrad [1] to Bayesian demonstration selection in DSPy MIPROv2 [2] and reflective trajectory mutators like GEPA, automated prompt optimization is transitioning from academic curiosity into core infrastructure for production agent platforms.

From Heuristics to Natural Language Gradients

The fundamental obstacle to applying classical gradient descent to LLM prompts is discreteness: tokens are categorical integers, not differentiable floating-point weights. In classical machine learning, backpropagation computes the partial derivative of a scalar loss function with respect to every weight parameter. In a compound LLM system, what is the analog of a gradient when the parameters are English sentences?

The breakthrough arrived by recognizing that large language models are themselves capable of computing symbolic derivatives in natural language. In 2023, Google DeepMind's OPRO (Optimization by PROmpting) showed that an LLM could act as an optimizer by receiving a meta-prompt containing past candidate prompts and their evaluation scores, then proposing new candidates that improve upon previous iterations [4]. OPRO proved that LLMs could navigate discrete optimization landscapes on benchmarks like the Traveling Salesperson Problem and Big-Bench Hard.

However, OPRO treated the system as a black box: it knew only that a prompt scored 68% and another scored 72%. It lacked directional error attribution. In mid-2024, Stanford researchers introduced TextGrad [1], establishing a formal framework for automatic differentiation via natural language.

In TextGrad, an AI pipeline is represented as a computation graph where variables are text strings (prompts, intermediate responses, tool inputs) and operations are LLM calls, deterministic code executions, or objective evaluations. When an objective function (such as an LLM judge or unit test suite) assesses the output, it does not merely emit a scalar score; it produces a detailed textual critique explaining precisely why the output failed. TextGrad then backpropagates this critique upstream through the computation graph. An LLM acts as the gradient operator, translating the downstream criticism into an actionable update direction for the upstream prompt [1]. Just as PyTorch calls loss.backward(), TextGrad calls .backward() across language model invocations, updating system instructions to directly address the failure modes surfaced by the evaluation loss.

DSPy and Programmatic Compilation: MIPROv2

While TextGrad applies gradient-style feedback to arbitrary computation graphs, Stanford's DSPy framework took an orthogonal, modular approach: separating the program logic from prompt choices entirely [3]. In DSPy, an engineer writes declarative Python modules with typed signatures (such as Context, Question -> Answer or Trace, Plan -> ToolCall). The exact system instructions, prefix strings, and few-shot demonstrations are left unspecified.

Instead of writing prompts, the developer writes a training dataset of inputs and labels, defines an evaluation metric (which can range from exact match to an LLM-as-judge rubric), and invokes a DSPy compiler (teleprompter).

Early prompt optimizers like BootstrapFewShot simply searched for successful input-output examples to insert into the prompt. However, modern multi-agent systems require optimizing both the instructions and the in-context demonstrations simultaneously. This led to MIPRO (Multi-prompt Instruction Proposal and Optimization) and its 2025-2026 successor MIPROv2 [2].

MIPROv2 operates through a coordinated two-phase optimization loop: 1. Instruction Proposal: The optimizer analyzes the dataset, task signatures, and past execution traces. A proposal LLM generates 10 to 30 candidate system instructions for each module in the pipeline, drawing upon different reasoning styles, step-by-step scaffolds, and role definitions. 2. Bayesian Coordinate Search: With a discrete candidate space of instructions and a combinatorially vast pool of few-shot demonstrations, exhaustive grid search is impossible. MIPROv2 employs Tree-structured Parzen Estimators (TPE) or Bayesian optimization over surrogate models to sample configurations. In each iteration, it selects a joint set of instructions and few-shot exemplars across all pipeline modules, evaluates the configuration on a validation batch, and updates the surrogate model.

Empirical results across benchmarks like HotPotQA, GSM8K, and Iris showed that MIPROv2 consistently outperforms expert human prompt engineering by 8% to 19% absolute accuracy, while using smaller, cheaper foundation models to match or exceed unoptimized frontier model baselines [2].

Reflective Trajectory Optimization: How GEPA Mutates Agent Prompts

While MIPROv2 excels at structured, fixed-step pipelines (such as multi-hop RAG), autonomous agents present a unique challenge: dynamic loops. An agent executing bash commands, editing files, or calling enterprise APIs may run for thirty turns, experiencing intermittent tool failures, syntax errors, and context drift.

This environment birthed reflective trajectory optimizers, exemplified by GEPA (Genetic Evolutionary Prompt Architecture) and VeriEvolve [7]. Rather than treating the prompt as a static template tested against isolated questions, GEPA optimizes agent system prompts against full multi-turn execution trajectories.

When an agent fails a benchmark task in GEPA: 1. Trace Extraction: The runtime extracts the complete execution trace—every user message, tool invocation, shell error, and intermediate reasoning thought. 2. Reflective Attribution: A reflection agent analyzes the exact turning point where the agent lost momentum or made an invalid assumption. Did the agent hallucinate a CLI argument? Did it fail to check status_code before parsing JSON? Did it enter an infinite retry loop? 3. Genetic Mutation: The optimizer formulates targeted mutation instructions: "Add an explicit constraint requiring checking file existence with ls before executing rm." Using genetic crossover and mutation operators, the system spawns variants of the system prompt [6]. 4. Pareto Selection: The mutated prompts compete in an evolutionary pool against diverse validation environments. Candidates are ranked not only by task completion rate, but also by token efficiency, wall-clock latency, and tool-call safety [7].

Crucially, VeriEvolve [7] demonstrated in late 2026 that evolutionary prompt loops must optimize the evaluator alongside the agent. If the evaluator itself contains blind spots, the genetic optimizer will ruthlessly exploit them, discovering bizarre, convoluted prompts that game the benchmark without performing the real task.

A Worked Example: Debugging a Multi-Tenant Concierge

To see how automated prompt optimization behaves in production, consider a real-world scenario from an enterprise agentic concierge. A platform serves enterprise tenants across multiple sandboxes, executing API calls against customer databases. In early deployment, engineers noticed a recurring failure mode: during high-concurrency periods, non-production sandbox requests were leaking into production latency alerts, and agents were choosing deprecated API endpoints when uncertain.

A human engineer attempting to fix this wrote: "You are an enterprise concierge. Be careful to select only current API manifests. When calling tools, ensure sandbox boundaries are respected and do not call outdated versions."

When evaluated across a 200-task synthetic test harness, this human prompt achieved 71.5% task success and had a 12% false-attribution rate on multi-tenant identifiers.

The engineering team then set up a DSPy/GEPA optimization loop with an LLM judge scoring trajectory compliance. After 40 iterations over a 50-example calibration set, the optimizer generated a completely different instruction structure: markdown <operational_invariants> 1. Identify the caller scope (ims_org_id, sandbox_id, manifest_version) before selecting tool candidates. 2. If manifest contains multiple versions, query the default version metadata explicitly. Never infer defaults from sandbox name. 3. On tool timeout (>10s), classify failure as infrastructure throttle; do not retry with alternate endpoint without caller confirmation. </operational_invariants> <execution_scaffold> Plan: [Verify scope] -> [Resolve version] -> [Call endpoint] -> [Validate response schema]

This computer-optimized prompt improved task completion to 89.2% and reduced cross-sandbox leaks to zero. The optimizer discovered that prefixing execution with structured tags (<operational_invariants>) and forcing a rigid three-token verification pattern drastically reduced token probability drift in mid-sized LLMs—a formatting quirk that human engineers rarely discover by intuition.

The Overfitting Trap: Reward Hacking and Runaway Tokens

Automated prompt optimization is not a silver bullet; it introduces vulnerabilities analogous to overfitting in deep neural networks:

  1. Judge Exploitation and Style Bias: As demonstrated in extensive audits of LLM judges [8], language models exhibit strong verbosity and authority biases. If the objective function uses an LLM-as-judge without chance-corrected calibration (such as Cohen's kappa), the prompt optimizer quickly discovers that adding polite academic jargon, exhaustive summaries, and multi-paragraph disclaimers boosts judge scores—even when the factual output is identical or worse.
  2. Context Window Bloat: Unconstrained prompt optimizers tend to accumulate instructions monotonically. Each failed edge case spawns another defensive clause, ballooning system prompts from 400 tokens to 4,000 tokens. This increases inference latency, compounds token costs across every multi-turn step, and triggers the "lost in the middle" degradation phenomenon in attention layers.
  3. Train-to-Test Distribution Shifts: A prompt hyper-optimized on twenty synthetic edge cases frequently degrades on mundane, daily queries. In benchmark studies, prompts that gained +15% on a narrow training set lost 6% to 11% when evaluated on real user traffic distributions [8].

Practical Playbook: Guidelines for Agent Teams

To deploy automated prompt optimization effectively without falling into the overfitting trap, leading agent teams adhere to five operational rules:

  1. Freeze the Evaluator First: Never optimize prompts against an uncalibrated evaluation metric. Run metamorphic tests (like SANE) and human-agreement checks on your evaluation harness before letting an automated optimizer run wild [7, 8].
  2. Enforce Strict Token Budgets: Set hard upper bounds on instruction length in the optimizer's loss function (e.g., penalizing prompts exceeding 800 words). Efficiency-constrained optimizers generate denser, clearer instructions.
  3. Use Holdout Test Partitions: Maintain strict separation between training prompts, validation tuning, and held-out test suites. If a prompt gains 10% on train but fails to generalize on test, reject the mutation.
  4. Log Traces, Not Just Outputs: Feed the optimizer execution traces—including tool arguments, schema validation errors, and intermediate reasoning—rather than just the final answer string.
  5. Human-in-the-Loop Sanity Review: Never deploy an auto-generated prompt directly to production without human inspection. Generated prompts can occasionally introduce subtle hallucinations or unintended security bypasses.

As models evolve throughout 2026, the boundary between prompt optimization and parameter fine-tuning is dissolving. Furthermore, self-optimizing runtimes are entering production environments: agents that continuously capture production execution failures, cluster them into failure archetypes, and automatically propose canary prompt revisions overnight. For any team building production LLM agents, automated prompt optimization is no longer optional—it is the compiler that turns raw foundation models into reliable enterprise software.
