When a Citation Looks Right but Doesn’t Prove the Claim

A practical guide to testing source-grounded answers, from retrieval through claim-level verification.

Executive Takeaways

  • A working, relevant link does not show that the cited passage supports the exact claim, that the answer is complete, or that the model relied on that source.
  • Split answers into atomic claims and measure support, coverage, factual correctness, completeness, and provenance separately.
  • LLM judges need human calibration, disagreement review, and their own error tracking; treat them as scalable triage rather than ground truth.
  • Counterfactual source changes can test whether an answer responds to evidence, while preserving the limits of what such probes can reveal.
Strategic Context: This connects directly to your interest in agent evaluation, observability, and source attribution: it turns citations into a measurable evidence trail.

A polished answer with footnotes can still leave the reader unable to check what matters: whether each important statement is actually supported by the source placed beside it. That gap is becoming more visible as assistants shift from short answers to “deep research” reports. A 2026 preprint that tested 14 web research models found a striking separation between citations that worked and looked relevant, and citations that supported the attached claim. The lesson is practical: citation quality is not one score, and a link is only the beginning of verification. [12]

This deep dive follows the whole evidence path: finding useful material, converting it into claims, assigning sources, and checking the finished answer. The research has made real progress on each step, but its tests answer different questions. A good evaluation therefore needs a small portfolio of checks, a representative test set, and human review focused on the places automated judges are least dependable.

A citation has at least four jobs

First, the reference must exist and open. A syntactically plausible URL can be fabricated, mistyped, redirected to an unrelated page, or inaccessible to the reader. A working page is a baseline integrity check, not evidence that the text above it is right. A 2024 reporting test of AI search tools also found that a citation could point to an accessible copy or aggregator while missing the original reporting users expected to verify. [13]

Second, the page must be relevant to the topic. An article about the same company or medical condition may not say anything about the specific number, date, causal claim, or comparison in the answer. Topical similarity can fool both a reader skimming a page title and a model scoring embeddings.

Third, the source must support the exact claim. “The report discusses battery fires” does not support “battery failures rose 32% last year.” Support is a relation between a proposition and evidence, not a property of a URL. The strongest practical unit is an atomic claim paired with a short source passage: who did what, when, where, under what conditions, and with what qualification. A foundational framework called Attributable to Identified Sources formalizes the human test as whether a reviewer would judge the statement true given its cited evidence. [15]

Fourth, the attribution should describe where the answer’s evidence came from. That is harder than checking whether a citation could support a sentence. A model might know a fact from training, write it from memory, then attach a source that happens to agree. The citation is correct in isolation, but the output has not demonstrated that it used the source. [10]

These checks also sit beside two separate answer qualities: factual correctness and completeness. A claim can accurately restate one document while the document itself is wrong or outdated. Conversely, a response may be true but omit the main caveat or a contradictory result. “Grounded” should mean supported by the available evidence; it should not silently mean globally true, current, unbiased, or complete.

A helpful analogy is a lab notebook. A result needs a legible source, a relevant page, and a trace from each conclusion back to the observation that supports it. A citation list at the end resembles a shelf of books in the lab: useful, but it does not show which measurement supports which conclusion. The claim-to-evidence map is the working record.

Measure claims, not citation counts

The early benchmark ALCE made citation evaluation concrete by measuring answer correctness, fluency, and citation quality separately. It assembled questions, retrieved evidence, and long-form answers with citations; on its ELI5 test set, even the strongest tested systems lacked complete citation support half the time. That is a result about those systems and that benchmark, not a universal current failure rate. But it established an important design point: a readable answer can still be incompletely supported. [1]

For a simple report, take the sentence: “The agency approved the project on Tuesday, adding 40 miles of track and reducing the trip by 18 minutes.” That is at least four claims: which agency, which approval date, how much track, and which trip-time comparison. One citation might support the date and mileage but not the travel-time estimate. If the whole sentence receives one pass/fail label, the system hides the partial failure. Split it into atomic statements; attach one or more evidence passages to each; then judge each pair.

AttributionBench tested whether language models could perform that judging task. Even fine-tuned GPT-3.5 reached about 80% macro-F1 on a binary attribution classification setup. The authors inspected more than 300 errors and found many came from nuance and mismatches between what the model judge could see and the information available to human annotators. CiteME measures a different neighboring task—recovering the paper behind a scientific claim—and found 4.2%–18.5% accuracy for tested language models versus 69.7% for human annotators; those numbers concern citation retrieval, not claim-support judging. [7] That is a warning against treating an LLM judge’s score as ground truth. It also shows why the benchmark should include “partly supported,” “contradicted,” “ambiguous,” and “insufficient evidence” cases, rather than training everything into a clean yes/no. [6]

A practical scorecard can keep the dimensions visible:

  • Link validity: does the canonical URL load, and does it still resolve to the intended source?
  • Relevance: is the page about the subject and event in the claim?
  • Claim support: does the cited passage entail the claim, contradict it, or leave it unresolved?
  • Coverage: are all material claims attributed, including figures, dates, exceptions, and comparisons?
  • Answer correctness and completeness: is the overall response accurate and responsive to the question, including important qualifications?
  • Provenance faithfulness: is there evidence the system relied on the cited source, rather than adding the link after composing from memory?

Do not average these into one headline metric too early. A system that returns 99% working links and 55% claim support is not “77% reliable” in a useful sense. The low dimension can be hidden by the high one, and teams may optimize the wrong stage. Keep counts and error examples for each dimension, and report confidence intervals when comparing systems.

Retrieval and answer writing fail in different places

RAG pipelines have several moving parts: document extraction, chunking, search, ranking, synthesis, and attribution. If the answer lacks evidence, the retrieval stage may have failed to find it. If the evidence was present but the model ignored it, generation or context use is the likely failure. If it found the right document but attached the wrong URL, citation rendering is at fault. One end-to-end score rarely tells an engineer which component to change. A 2025 survey that reviewed 582 RAG papers groups evaluation into internal component checks and external system properties, underscoring how broad the measurement problem has become. [14]

ARES was designed to evaluate context relevance, answer faithfulness, and answer relevance as distinct properties. It trains lightweight evaluators on synthetic data, then uses a small human-labeled sample with prediction-powered inference to correct aggregate estimates. The authors report using a few hundred human annotations across eight knowledge-intensive tasks. The approach makes evaluation more scalable than manually labeling every run, while retaining a human anchor for the estimate. Its limit is that calibration quality depends on whether the labeled sample represents the production questions and failure cases. [3]

RAGAS takes a related reference-free route: it evaluates retrieval and answer behavior without requiring a gold answer for every test. Its metric families include context precision and recall, faithfulness, and response relevancy. These are useful diagnostics, but “faithfulness to retrieved context” is not the same as “the cited source is authoritative and factually correct.” A response can faithfully repeat a bad passage; a system can also omit a key fact that was never retrieved. Metric names need local definitions and validation against the product’s actual decisions. [2]

RAGChecker pushes further toward diagnosis, separating retrieval and generation with fine-grained metrics and testing the framework against human judgments. Its evaluation of eight RAG systems is a useful example of why one aggregate score is too blunt: architecture choices create trade-offs, and the same system can retrieve broadly yet synthesize poorly. It remains a framework’s own meta-evaluation, so independent checks on a new domain matter before trusting its ranking. [4]

The 2025 TREC RAG track turns that separation into a shared evaluation exercise. It moved toward longer, multi-sentence narrative information needs and assessed relevance, response completeness, attribution verification, and agreement analysis across more than 150 submissions. The more realistic query shape matters: real users often ask for a joined explanation, not one lookup. But shared test collections still define a boundary. Success on one corpus and topic distribution does not establish robust behavior on live, changing, multilingual, or private documents. [11]

This suggests a useful debugging sequence. Preserve the retrieved passages for each run. Score whether the needed evidence was present and ranked highly enough. Then test whether each generated claim follows from its cited passage. Finally score answer-level correctness and coverage against a reference or expert rubric. If the retrieval set lacks evidence, improve search and indexing. If evidence is present but the claim overreaches, inspect generation prompts, citation assignment, and conflict handling. If correctness is poor even with sound sources, the issue may be synthesis, not search.

The judge needs its own evaluation

Most scalable citation checks rely on a second model to decide whether one passage supports one claim. That judge can miss negation, conditions, dates, table notes, and disagreement across documents. It may also prefer longer answers, share the generator’s blind spots, or reward a familiar claim even when the evidence does not say it.

SourceCheckup, a 2025 medical-domain study, illustrates both the value and limits of this approach. Its agent-based evaluation examined seven models on 800 questions and 58,000 statement-source pairs. The authors reported that 50%–90% of model responses were not fully supported, and that around 30% of GPT-4o with web search’s individual statements were unsupported; nearly half of its responses were not fully supported. They validated stages of the automated pipeline and its source verifier with practicing physicians. These numbers are concerning, but they describe a particular medical benchmark and evaluation setup. They should not be projected onto every domain or current product. [9]

The study also points to an evaluator’s own error budget. Question generation can produce poor prompts; claim parsing can split or omit a statement; URL retrieval can fail; the judge can misread evidence; and a human reference label can be ambiguous. Track these stages separately. A final “unsupported” label without a reason code is hard to act on and can turn parser defects into apparent model defects.

ARES offers one response: calibrate automated judgments against human annotations, then estimate the overall score with statistical correction. AttributionBench gives another: deliberately construct difficult cases and analyze disagreement rather than simply reporting accuracy. A newer deep-research evaluation paper explicitly calibrates its fact-check judge through manual review of 50–100 judgments and acknowledges position and self-enhancement bias as limitations. The common thread is that evaluators need an audit trail and a measured agreement rate, not just a prompt that says “be rigorous.” [3][6][12]

A good evaluation set should include adversarial but realistic pairs: a relevant page with the wrong number; a correct fact with an outdated source; a source that supports the subject but not the causal explanation; a table whose footnote reverses the interpretation; two credible sources that disagree; a conditional result reported without the condition; and a claim that must be refused because no evidence is available. Have domain reviewers label a sample, document the rubric, measure agreement, and review the disagreements. Use the judge as a triage tool until it has shown dependable performance on that task.

A correct citation can still be post-hoc

Citation correctness asks whether the evidence supports the claim. Citation faithfulness asks whether the source actually informed the answer. These are not interchangeable. A model can correctly state that Berlin is Germany’s capital from memory and then cite a travel page that says the same thing. A source checker sees a match; a user may infer that the answer was derived from the cited page. That inference is not justified by a match alone.

Wallat and colleagues formalized this distinction and used adversarial contexts to expose post-rationalization: an answer may remain tied to parametric knowledge even when the retrieved document is altered or conflicts with it. They report that up to 57% of citations in their tested setup lacked faithfulness. The “up to” is essential. It is a result under their experimental design, not a general percentage of citations across all deployed RAG systems. [10]

How can a system test reliance? Use counterfactuals. If a source is the only evidence for a detail, remove that detail and see if the answer changes or abstains. Replace it with a plausible alternative and check whether the answer follows the changed evidence. Add a source that contradicts the model’s prior and check whether it handles the conflict explicitly. Compare answers when evidence is withheld, reordered, or made irrelevant. These are causal probes of context use, not proof of a model’s private reasoning, but they reveal whether the output is sensitive to the evidence it claims to rely on.

Generation design influences the result. ALCE treats retrieval and answer writing as an end-to-end task; Self-RAG trains a model to retrieve when needed and reflect on retrieved passages and drafts; LAB compares generation with evidence extraction in one step against approaches that add retrieval. On LAB’s six long-document tasks and five models, one-step citation worked best for larger or fine-tuned models, while added retrieval helped smaller prompted models. There is no single universally best placement for citation logic: the right choice depends on model capability, document length, and task. [1][5][8]

A robust product can nevertheless enforce an observable contract: draft a claim, identify the source span, attach the citation, then run a verification pass before display. Whether these occur in one model call or multiple stages is an implementation choice; the evaluation should inspect the trace. Preserve retrieved text and span identifiers, keep citations attached to the specific sentences they support, and record whether the claim was generated before or after evidence selection. If the user cannot inspect the passage, the system should not imply stronger provenance than it can demonstrate.

A worked evaluation, end to end

Suppose an assistant answers: “A city council approved a 40-mile rail extension on Tuesday, and the trip will be 18 minutes shorter.” The answer contains several claims, and the likely evidence comes from a council vote report, a project fact sheet, and a travel-time model. A production test can proceed like this:

  1. Freeze the question and sources. Record the exact question, retrieval query, source URLs, fetch time, and content hashes. That makes later comparisons meaningful if a news page is edited or disappears.
  2. Check the retrieval result. Did the council report appear? Was it ranked above opinion or an old project page? Was the travel-time model retrieved at all? This separates missing evidence from a generation mistake.
  3. Parse the answer into claims. Keep the vote date, route length, and travel-time change separate. Preserve qualifiers such as “projected,” “weekday,” or “after phase two”; a claim splitter must not erase them.
  4. Pair claims with passages. The vote page may support “approved Tuesday” but not “40 miles.” The project page may specify 38.6 miles, which rounds to 39 rather than 40. The forecast may compare end-to-end times only after a later phase. Mark each claim supported, contradicted, incomplete, or unknown, with the exact passage and reason.
  5. Check answer-level quality. If two of three claims are supported, the paragraph should not pass simply because most of its words are sourced. The “18 minutes shorter” claim may be valid only under a scenario; the final answer must retain that condition. Score factual correctness and completeness separately from support.
  6. Probe faithfulness. Remove the forecast from context. Does the model still produce “18 minutes”? If so, the citation may be post-hoc or the model may be drawing on other evidence. Replace “18” with “12” in a controlled copy; does the answer report the changed evidence or cling to its original? Record this as a counterfactual sensitivity test, not as a claim that the model exposes its internal reasoning.
  7. Audit the auditor. Have a reviewer label the pairs without seeing the generator’s identity, compare with the automated judge, and inspect disagreements. If the judge misses the “phase two” condition, add that pattern to the test set and adjust the rubric or evaluator.

This example demonstrates why a single “groundedness: 0.82” number can mislead. Better reporting includes claim-support rate, material-claim coverage, wrong or broken URL rate, answer correctness, and the percentage of cases where the answer changes appropriately under evidence interventions. Show both aggregate results and representative failures. When the score changes after a new prompt or retriever, engineers can see which layer improved and which one regressed.

A production evaluation that can guide changes

Start with a frozen core set of questions that represents actual use, not only easy demonstrations. Include simple fact retrieval, multi-hop synthesis, current information, conflicting sources, long documents, tables and charts, ambiguous wording, and unanswerable questions. Add new examples from real failures, but keep a stable holdout so the team can tell whether a change generalizes instead of merely memorizing yesterday’s bugs.

Label the expected facts and evidence spans at the level needed for the decision. A gold answer does not need to be a single canonical paragraph; it can define required claims, acceptable variants, must-preserve qualifications, and source quality rules. For fresh-web tasks, store snapshots or hashes plus retrieval time. A live URL alone is a moving target, and archived pages can become unavailable. The deep-research preprint highlights this temporal instability and measures URL availability separately from topical relevance and fact support. [12]

Then measure a layered set of outcomes:

  • Retrieval: evidence recall and ranking quality; whether the right passage was present in the context the model actually saw.
  • Attribution: support precision (how many cited claim-pairs are genuinely supported) and coverage (how many important claims have adequate evidence). Penalize unsupported specificity and irrelevant citation piles.
  • Answer: factual correctness, completeness, contradiction handling, and appropriate abstention.
  • Provenance: source quality, accessibility, publication time, and counterfactual sensitivity to evidence changes.
  • Operations: latency, cost, timeout and fetch-failure rates, and judge-human disagreement.

There is a real trade-off between citation density and readability. Adding a citation after every clause can create noisy text and more chances for mismatching. Too few citations leave readers unable to see where a conclusion came from. Evaluate citation granularity against the user’s verification task: one source may support a paragraph of one source’s summary, but a sentence combining two studies usually needs distinct attribution. The best granularity is the smallest span that lets a reader check the meaningful claim without reconstructing the whole report.

Finally, evaluate changes with paired comparisons and uncertainty estimates. Compare two versions on the same questions and source snapshots; review a blinded sample of disagreements. Report results by domain, answer length, source type, and failure class so an overall gain does not conceal a serious regression. Use a statistical correction or bootstrap when the test set is small. Keep a calibrated human sample in the loop, especially when the judge, generator, and retrieval stack share a model family.

What to watch next

The field is moving from “did the answer include citations?” toward “can a parser recover every claim-source pair, fetch the cited text, and verify the actual support?” The 2025 TREC design, SourceCheckup’s statement-source data, and the 2026 deep-research preprint all point toward layered, pairwise evaluation. The preprint’s 130 multi-source research queries and three-stage checks make it particularly relevant to research agents, but it is still a preprint, its judge is model-based, and its model lineup and task design bound what the results can establish. [9][11][12]

Watch whether future benchmarks publish stable source snapshots, test citation faithfulness with counterfactual evidence, and report judge calibration across domains. Watch whether systems expose claim-level evidence spans instead of only clickable document lists. And watch for careful separation of support, truth, completeness, and source reliability in product metrics.

For builders, the near-term goal is not a perfect universal judge. It is a visible chain from question to retrieved passage to claim to citation, with each link testable and each unresolved judgment labeled honestly. A dependable source-grounded assistant should make it cheaper to verify an answer, easy to spot what is uncertain, and hard for an attractive citation to conceal a weak claim.
