Jensen Huang Exits Nvidia as Wall Street Tests a $500 Billion AI Financing Web

Sources: NVIDIA Newsroom / CNBC / PBS NewsHour • NVIDIA Newsroom — Statement From the Independent Members of the NVIDIA Board (Nov 10, 2026) • CNBC — Jensen Huang's Nvidia Exit Casts Shadow Over Wall Street's $500 Billion AI Financing Bet • CNBC — Nvidia Live Updates: Huang Agrees to Testify Before Congress; Kress Named Interim CEO • CNBC — Interim CEO Colette Kress and Board Chair Harvey Jones Emails to Nvidia Employees • CNBC — Ex-Prosecutor Joon Kim and Wachtell, Lipton Investigation Into Nvidia • PBS NewsHour / Associated Press — Sharp Swings for Nvidia and Oil Prices Weigh on Wall Street

At dawn in Santa Clara on Thursday, November 12, the longest-serving founder tenure in big technology ended effective immediately. Nvidia's board of directors announced that 63-year-old co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as president, chief executive officer, and board member. Across thirty-three years since co-founding the company in 1993, Huang steered Nvidia from near-bankruptcy in the late 1990s into a $4 trillion compute giant after a 1,722% four-year stock surge. Executive Vice President and Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress—who joined Nvidia in September 2013 after nearly twenty-five finance years at Microsoft and Cisco—takes over immediately as Interim CEO while retaining her finance post during an internal and external search. Alongside her, longtime director and former Synopsys chief Harvey C. Jones becomes the first Chair of the Board in Nvidia's history. Management confirmed Nvidia will still report third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on Tuesday, November 17 at 1:20 PM Pacific Time, followed by a company-wide employee meeting the next morning at 9:00 AM.

Wall Street met the news with violent two-way volume. Nvidia shares plunged 14% at Thursday's opening bell to a fresh 52-week low, deepening a roughly 27% slide from last month's peak. Buyers stepped in steadily through the afternoon to pare the closing loss to 4.3%, leaving Nvidia narrowly above the $4 trillion mark. Vanda Research recorded the largest retail opening bid in market history. Individual traders bought $111 million net of Nvidia stock in the first thirty minutes—forty times a normal open—after pouring $42.5 million into two-times leveraged Nvidia funds earlier in the week. Institutional desks moved the other way. Fund managers rotated sharply into rival silicon designers Broadcom and AMD, lifting both between 5% and 6%, while Alphabet gained 2%. Meanwhile, neocloud partner CoreWeave dropped 10% and server maker Super Micro Computer tumbled nearly 12%. How did a company with multi-quarter hardware backlogs unravel at the top in seventy-two hours? Five developments tell the story.

Number one is the independent board action that made Huang's departure unavoidable. On Tuesday, November 10, Nvidia's independent directors retained Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz alongside former Acting Manhattan U.S. Attorney Joon H. Kim to investigate executive conduct, governance controls, and potential national-security exposure. That mandate followed Huang's public disclosure on November 8 of a personal relationship with an overseas cloud executive whom U.S. intelligence officials suspect of ties to foreign intelligence. Board sentiment turned decisive late Wednesday night after The New York Times reported an undisclosed relationship with an internal vice president and Reuters reported a second undisclosed relationship with another Nvidia employee. For any $4 trillion public company, undisclosed subordinate relationships cross an unwaivable fiduciary and human-resources line. Huang has retained outside counsel at Paul, Weiss, Rifkind, Wharton & Garrison while Kim's investigators examine internal communications, reporting lines, and corporate security protocols.

Second, corporate registry filings have widened an internal board inquiry into a cross-border supply-chain and export-routing audit. Private intelligence firm Tesari shared filings with CNBC this morning tracing Singapore cloud executive Alice Huang's corporate web across East and Southeast Asia. Those records show she sold an earlier cloud company, Osmanthus, in 2019 to Chinese gaming firm 7Road. Its Singapore spin-off, Megaspeed, subsequently facilitated at least $7.5 billion in Nvidia chip shipments to Chinese buyers—a disclosure that sent reported Megaspeed customer Alibaba down roughly 7% in Hong Kong trading. Tesari also traced co-ownership stakes through Shanghai Hexi and Shanghai Century Longteng into Shenzhen Yifeng Technology, a mainland data-center operator whose anchor client was Huawei Cloud, backed by corporate facility loan guarantees. Adding to the legal pressure, Bloomberg reported that federal prosecutors in the Southern District of New York are probing whether related intermediaries helped route Super Micro AI servers into China.

Third, bipartisan committee leaders in Washington moved within hours to put both export controls and corporate oversight under oath. House Foreign Affairs Chair Brian Mast announced Thursday that Jensen Huang agreed to testify voluntarily before Congress ahead of Thanksgiving. Over in the Senate, Banking Chair Tim Scott demanded public testimony from Huang on December 3 and from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on December 10. Scott warned that formal subpoenas will follow if either witness declines to appear. Incoming House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries simultaneously requested a classified intelligence briefing for congressional leadership. At the same time, Senate Finance Democrat Ron Wyden and House Ways and Means Democrat Richard Neal launched a formal inquiry into December 2025 export rules. Those rules allowed Nvidia to ship Taiwanese-fabricated H200 accelerators to Chinese buyers under a 25% tariff.

Fourth—and most critical for capital markets—is the $500 billion private-credit architecture Huang was personally negotiating on Wall Street. In August, Nvidia signed preliminary memorandums of understanding with six financial heavyweights: Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR. Their goal was mobilizing over half a trillion dollars in private debt collateralized directly by Nvidia graphics clusters. Look inside Nvidia's second-quarter Form 10-Q from July and substantial balance-sheet commitments are already live. Nvidia carried $36 billion in off-balance-sheet debt backstops for neocloud operators alongside $20 billion in direct data-center lease commitments. Even larger was a reported $105 billion backstop proposed in August for OpenAI's massive Ohio data-center campus. Crucially, internal reporting shows Colette Kress had resisted that $105 billion guarantee for months, pressing commercial teams to replace non-binding letters of intent with legally executed customer offtake contracts before pledging Nvidia's credit.

And fifth, this transition forces a structural overhaul of a boardroom that governance scholars like Yale's Jeffrey Sonnenfeld long criticized as a founder monarchy. Until today, Nvidia's eleven-member board operated with no chairperson at all and zero sitting or former S&P 500 chief executives among its ten independent directors. Director tenures averaged fifteen years—nearly double the 7.8-year S&P 500 norm—while Huang's two adult children, Madison and Spencer, alongside son-in-law Nico Caprez as Vice President of Global AI Infrastructure, held internal leadership posts. Sell-side desks moved quickly to price in transition risk. Cantor Fitzgerald's CJ Muse cut his price target from $350 to $250, KeyBanc's John Vinh flagged a higher organizational risk premium, and Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon warned of protracted multi-agency inquiries as Rosen Law Firm filed an initial shareholder suit. One ownership detail tempers fears of disorderly selling, however. Huang's roughly $140 billion equity stake sits largely inside family trusts co-controlled with his wife Lori, preventing sudden open-market liquidation.

Step back from Thursday's market swings and separate personality from engineering. Does losing an iconic founder break Nvidia's competitive moat? History across Silicon Valley's trillion-dollar handoffs—from Apple in 2011 to Microsoft in 2014—shows durable dominance rests on architecture and talent depth rather than one dealmaker. One executive did not write CUDA's software libraries alone. Nor did one person tape out Blackwell rack interconnects or lock in leading-edge wafer and high-bandwidth memory allocations across Taiwan and South Korea through 2028. In internal emails sent to staff on Thursday morning, Kress and Jones emphasized continuity across engineering teams whose chip roadmaps already span multiple future generations. Yes, cloud hyperscalers were already hedging vendor lock-in across Broadcom custom ASICs, AMD clusters, and open Ethernet fabrics. Capitol Hill's export hearings will certainly nudge enterprise procurement committees further down that multi-vendor path at the margin.

Here is what we make of it—and why Wall Street's opening panic misread the AI credit cycle. For the past eighteen months, the sharpest systemic vulnerability in artificial intelligence has not been compute demand. It has been circular vendor financing, off-balance-sheet neocloud guarantees, and $500 billion private-debt proposals backed by fast-depreciating silicon. Elevating a battle-tested chief financial officer like Colette Kress alongside an independent board chair in Harvey Jones directly targets that weak link. Why does that distinction matter for long-term builders and investors? If Kress cools off $100 billion balance-sheet backstops and insists on binding, cash-backed customer contracts over loose letters of intent, thinly capitalized GPU clouds will face a healthy reckoning while the core AI buildout grows sturdier and far more bankable. What would change our judgment? Only evidence on Tuesday's November 17 earnings call of hyperscaler Blackwell order deferrals, senior engineering defections, or federal injunctions halting international shipments would turn a necessary governance reset into a structural thesis break.

Nvidia's leadership transition did not unfold in a vacuum—it collided with a bond market repricing interest rates and energy risk across every asset class, which is right where we head next.

Act I: Photons Under Eight Kilometers of Water and the First Petabit Cable

Sources: Engineering at Meta & TeleGeography Submarine Cable 101 • TeleGeography — Submarine Cable 101: Global Fleet, Physical Anatomy & Satellite Capacity Comparison

Picture an ordinary garden hose lying in pitch darkness seven thousand meters beneath the North Atlantic. Strip away its outer jacket. Across thousands of kilometers of deep ocean basin, an intercontinental fiber-optic cable is no thicker than that backyard hose. At its center sit ultra-pure silica glass filaments measuring exactly 125 micrometers across, the width of a human hair. Sealed inside water-blocking gel and a welded steel tube, those fibers carry infrared laser pulses at two-thirds the speed of light in a vacuum.

Around that central steel tube sit high-tensile steel strength wires, a swaged copper sleeve carrying high-voltage direct current from shore, and high-density polyethylene insulation. Only on shallow continental shelves do engineers wrap the line in double steel-wire or rock armor and plow it beneath the seabed. Why keep the deep-sea line so slender while armoring the coasts? Deep-sea sharks make great viral videos. Yet International Cable Protection Committee logs recorded zero shark-bite faults between 2007 and 2014. Commercial fishing trawlers and dragged ship anchors on shallow coastal shelves cause almost every mechanical break. Where water grows shallow, steel armor takes over.

For nearly four decades, engineers multiplied the capacity of those glass strands through two breakthroughs. In the late 1980s, erbium-doped fiber amplifiers arrived on the seabed. Laser-pumped erbium ions boosted dimmed 1.55-micrometer light directly in the optical domain without slow electronic conversion. Coherent dispersion-uncompensated optics followed in the 2010s and unlocked another tenfold leap in bandwidth. Soon afterward, single-core fibers slammed straight into Claude Shannon's nonlinear information-capacity ceiling. Optical physics hit a wall.

Pack more laser power into one microscopic core and the light distorts itself. Push higher electrical current from shore to feed extra repeaters and you breach coastal voltage limits. Engineers pivoted to Spatial Division Multiplexing instead. They packed more fiber pairs inside one narrow housing while sharing pump lasers across pairs. Transatlantic designs climbed steadily from Marea's eight fiber pairs at 224 terabits per second to Amitie's sixteen pairs and Anjana's twenty-four pairs at half a petabit.

Now that architecture has crossed a historic milestone. On September 21, Meta joined NEC, Sumitomo Electric Industries, and Orange to unveil Petal. Stretching 7,000 kilometers—roughly 4,300 miles—between the United States and France's Atlantic coast, the cable enters service in 2029. Petal is the world's first petabit-class transoceanic cable, carrying 1,000 terabits per second. That delivers 5.5 times Marea's capacity, or enough bandwidth for 6.25 billion people—three-quarters of humanity—to stream 160-kilobit audio at once.

How did engineers double capacity without swelling the hull to forty-eight fiber pairs or exceeding 18-kilovolt shore power limits? Sumitomo Electric engineered its 2C Z-PLUS ultra-low-loss fiber from a twenty-centimeter-wide synthetic silica preform containing two distinct light-guiding cores. Drawn down into standard 125-micrometer cladding, those cores run optical signals in opposite directions with elevated refractive indices so crosstalk drops to virtually zero. Roughly one hundred single-body seabed repeaters use optical Fan-In and Fan-Out splitters to feed ninety-six shared amplifiers inside existing Power Feeding Equipment limits. Clever glass physics outsmarted deep-sea electrical constraints—yet physics alone cannot protect where those fibers meet the shore.
