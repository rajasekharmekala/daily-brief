LLM judges flip verdicts on reruns and answer-order swaps; one fully repeatable judge was right only 51% of the time

A reliability audit of LLM-as-a-judge runs six frontier models across four benchmarks, five prompt formats, two presentation orders, three temperatures and ten repeats. Verdicts change between identical runs even at temperature zero, swapping answer order flips most verdicts on hard tasks, and one perfectly repeatable judge matched ground truth only 51% of the time. The authors propose a trustworthy verdict rate and find holistic rubric scoring beats pairwise win-rate prompting.

Pachi Mirapa Guddu Vepudu (Andhra Green-Chilli Egg Fry)పచ్చిమిర్చి గుడ్డు వేపుడు

Boiled eggs blistered golden in groundnut oil, then tossed in a fiery coriander and green-chilli masala that clings like a dry curry. It is the green-chilli cousin of the usual red karam egg fry: sharper, fresher and ready in about 20 minutes.

  • 5.4 g net carbs
  • 20 g protein
  • 26 g fat
  • 340 kcal
  • 22 min
  • Serves 2

Ingredients
  • large eggs, hard-boiled, peeled and halved lengthwise6 eggs · 300 g
  • groundnut oil1 tbsp · 14 g
  • ghee1 tsp · 5 g
  • green chillies, 18 g blended and 4 g slitabout 6 Indian green chillies · 22 g
  • fresh coriander leaves and tender stems1 small bunch · 20 g
  • onion, very finely chopped2 tbsp · 20 g
  • garlic cloves1 large clove · 5 g
  • ginger1 cm piece · 4 g
  • curry leaves1 sprig · 2 g
  • mustard seeds1/3 tsp · 1 g
  • cumin seeds1/3 tsp · 1 g
  • turmeric powdera pinch · 0.5 g
  • black pepper, freshly crushedabout 1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • lemon juice1 tsp · 5 g
  • saltabout 1/3 tsp · 2 g
Method
  1. Lower the eggs into a pan of boiling water and cook 9 minutes. Move them straight into iced water for 3 minutes, peel, halve lengthwise and pat very dry. Dry surfaces blister; wet ones steam. Prick each yolk side once or twice with a fork so the masala can cling.
  2. While the eggs cook, blend 18 g of the green chillies, the coriander, garlic, ginger and 1 tablespoon of water into a coarse, bright-green paste. Slit the remaining 4 g chilli lengthwise.
  3. Heat the groundnut oil in the skillet over medium-high heat. Add the curry leaves and slit chilli; they are ready when the leaves stop spluttering and turn glassy, about 30 seconds. Scoop them out and keep them for garnish.
  4. Lay the eggs in the pan cut-side down, sprinkle with the turmeric and a third of the salt, and leave them alone for 60 to 90 seconds until the whites are blistered and golden at the edges. Flip for 20 seconds, then lift the eggs onto a plate.
  5. Drop the heat to medium. Add the ghee, mustard seeds and cumin; when the mustard stops popping (about 20 seconds) add the onion and cook 2 minutes until translucent with golden edges.
  6. Tip in the green paste and the remaining salt. Fry 2 to 3 minutes, stirring, until the raw smell is gone, the colour dulls from bright to olive and a few drops of oil show at the edge of the pan. Keep the heat moderate so the green does not scorch and turn bitter.
  7. Return the eggs to the pan and fold gently for 1 to 2 minutes on medium-low until every piece is coated and the masala looks dry and clingy. Take off the heat, add the pepper and lemon juice, scatter the crisp curry leaves and chilli on top, and let it sit 2 minutes before serving.
Make it taste like home
  • Heat control: heat 4 as written. For heat 2, use 3 green chillies and de-seed them; for heat 5, add a spoon of finely chopped Thai bird chillies to the paste.
  • Keto: the paste carries the flavour, so you need only 20 g of onion for the whole pan. Most of the net carbs come from the green-chilli and aromatic paste; the eggs add under 2 g per serving.
  • Make ahead: boil and peel the eggs and blend the green paste up to 2 days ahead (paste in a jar under a film of oil); keep both refrigerated, covered; then breakfast is a 10-minute skillet job. Eat the same day; reheated eggs turn rubbery.
Swaps
  • Quail eggs (12 for 6 hen eggs) make a good party version.
  • Use all groundnut oil in place of ghee for a dairy-free dish.

Gongura Royyala Kura (Sorrel Prawn Masala)గోంగూర రొయ్యల కూర

Prawns seared hot, then folded into a thick olive-dark gongura masala and finished with a sizzling garlic and red-chilli talimpu. The sourness comes entirely from the leaf, so there is no tamarind and no jaggery.

  • 8.7 g net carbs
  • 23 g protein
  • 17 g fat
  • 289 kcal
  • 30 min
  • Serves 2
Fat 55%Protein 32%Net carbs 12%per serving
Ingredients
  • medium prawns, peeled and deveined (thawed if frozen)about 24 prawns · 300 g
  • gongura (sorrel) leaves, picked from stems, washed and fully dried, choppedabout 3 packed cups · 120 g
  • groundnut oil1 1/2 tbsp · 22 g
  • sesame oil, for the final talimpu1 1/2 tsp · 6 g
  • onion, finely choppeda quarter of a medium onion · 30 g
  • green chillies, slit3 chillies · 12 g
  • garlic, 4 g crushed with the ginger and 3 g sliced for the talimpu2 large cloves · 7 g
  • ginger1 cm piece · 4 g
  • Guntur karam (red chilli powder)1 1/2 tsp · 4.5 g
  • dried red chillies for the talimpu2 whole · 1.5 g
  • coriander powder1 tsp · 3 g
  • turmeric powder1/3 tsp · 0.8 g
  • curry leaves1 sprig · 2 g
  • cumin seeds1/3 tsp · 1 g
  • mustard seeds1/3 tsp · 1 g
  • cinnamon stick, a 1 cm piece0.4 g
  • green cardamom pods, lightly crushed2 pods · 0.5 g
  • saltabout 1/2 tsp · 3 g
Method
  1. Pat the prawns completely dry. Toss with the turmeric, a third of the salt and half of the Guntur karam; set aside while you prepare the gongura.
  2. Strip the gongura leaves from the stems, wash, and spin or pat them very dry (wet leaves stew instead of frying and dilute the sourness). Chop coarsely.
  3. Heat 1 tablespoon of the groundnut oil in a kadai over high heat until it shimmers. Sear the prawns in two batches, 45 to 60 seconds per side, just until they curl and turn pink on the outside. They will finish cooking later. Tip them onto a plate.
  4. Lower the heat to medium. Add the remaining groundnut oil, the cumin, mustard seeds, cinnamon and cardamom. When the mustard stops popping (about 20 seconds) add the onion, the slit green chillies and the curry leaves; cook 3 minutes until the onion is soft with golden edges.
  5. Stir in the crushed garlic and ginger for 45 seconds, then the remaining Guntur karam and the coriander powder with a splash of water so the spices do not burn. Add all the gongura and a pinch of salt. Cook 4 to 5 minutes, mashing with the back of a spoon, until the leaves collapse into a dark olive pulp and the oil starts to ooze back out at the edges.
  6. Add 3 tablespoons of water, scrape the pan and simmer 1 minute. Return the prawns and their juices, toss to coat, cover and cook on medium-low for 2 to 3 minutes, until the prawns are opaque and firm. Do not go longer or they turn rubbery.
  7. For the talimpu, heat the sesame oil in a small pan over medium heat. Add the dried red chillies and sliced garlic; when the garlic turns golden (about 30 seconds) pour the sizzling oil over the curry. Rest 2 minutes with the lid on, then taste and add salt or a squeeze more green chilli.

Method

Ingredient spotlight

Gongura (sorrel / roselle leaf): the Andhra sour that replaces tamarind

Gongura is the sour leaf behind some of Andhra's best-known flavours: sorrel or roselle, called ambadi in Maharashtra and pulicha keerai in Tamil Nadu. Its tang is clean and lemony, which is why Andhra cooks reach for it where others use tamarind. That is the keto point: tamarind is a carbohydrate trap, gongura is not. IFCT 2017 lists green gongura at 4.1 g available carbohydrate and 4.6 g fibre per 100 g, so I count 4.1 g net per 100 g, which comes to about 2.5 g per serving of today's prawn masala.

Two types turn up. Red-stemmed leaves are sharper and are usually chosen for pachadi; green-stemmed leaves are milder and suit curries. Wash the leaves, dry them thoroughly, strip them from the stems and cook them in oil until they collapse into a dark olive pulp; wet leaves just stew. Three cups of raw leaves cook down to roughly one cup of pulp.

Use it three ways: as the sour base of a prawn, chicken or mutton curry (today's lunch); fried and ground with roasted chillies and garlic into pachadi (see gongura pachadi and gongura kodi in the cookbook); or chopped into an egg fry or dal in place of lemon. Cooked pulp freezes in ice-cube trays for about a month, so one bunch covers several dinners. Like spinach, gongura contains oxalic acid, which is part of its sharp taste. On strict keto days, check the label on jarred gongura pickle first; carbohydrate varies by brand.

Where to find itIndian grocery stores such as Patel Brothers: fresh bunches (spring to autumn, labelled gongura, ambadi, pulicha keerai or sorrel/roselle leaves) in the fresh greens section, and frozen packs of cleaned or chopped leaves in the freezer aisle; South Asian grocers with online delivery also stock it.

Keto noteAt 4.1 g net carbohydrate per 100 g (IFCT 2017) a 60 g portion adds about 2.5 g net, far less than the roughly 6 g of carbohydrate in the 10 g of tamarind pulp that would stand in for it in a conventional recipe.

  • JWST rejected Cepheid crowding as the cause at 8.2σ; the main live dispute is which supernova calibrators are used, which is why the CCHP team's TRGB value (70.39 ± 1.9) differs from SH0ES (about 73).
  • TDCOSMO 2026, a ladder-free lensing result (71.1 +2.4/−2.3, preprint), says the tension with Planck persists at 2.7σ even with the whole local ladder removed.
  • Early dark energy and related sound-horizon fixes can shrink the gap on paper, but each pays in CMB spectra, lensing or structure growth; DESI's evolving dark-energy hint is not a direct fix.

For youCosmology is one of your stated interests, and the story is a case study in the same questions you work on in evaluation: correlated errors, blinding, subsample noise and leave-one-out robustness checks.

Ten days ago the TDCOSMO collaboration posted a measurement of the Hubble constant that skips the usual distance ladder: 71.1 (+2.4/−2.3) km/s/Mpc from 11 gravitationally lensed quasars, analysed blind [1]. The paper's main claim is that the mismatch with the cosmic-microwave-background (CMB) prediction survives even when the ladder is removed entirely. It arrives six months after a community "consensus" paper, written by more than 100 authors, put the local value at 73.50 ± 0.81 [2]. So a ladder-free result and a covariance-weighted ladder consensus are now both on the table. This piece explains what is being measured, where each number comes from, which "it is just an error" explanations have been tested, and which new-physics ideas are still standing.

Two ways to measure one number

The Hubble constant, H0, is the present-day expansion rate of the universe, quoted in kilometres per second per megaparsec (1 Mpc is about 3.26 million light-years). A galaxy 100 Mpc away should recede at about 100 × H0 km/s. Because 1/H0 sets the age scale, a higher H0 means a younger universe: roughly 13.3 billion years at 73.5 versus 14.5 at 67.4, before model-dependent corrections (my arithmetic).

The late route measures distances and velocities directly. The velocity of a distant galaxy is easy to read from its redshift. The distance is the hard part, and it is built in rungs, the "distance ladder". The NOIRLab illustration shows the idea [3].

The early route never measures H0 at all. The CMB is a snapshot taken about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, and its pattern of hot and cold spots contains sound waves from the early plasma. The angular size of those waves, a ratio of the "sound horizon" r_s (how far sound travelled before the plasma became transparent) to the distance to that last-scattering surface, is measured to 0.03% precision: 100θ* = 1.0411 ± 0.0003 in Planck's final analysis [4]. To get H0 you fit a six-parameter ΛCDM model (cold dark matter plus a cosmological constant). Planck's fit gives H0 = 67.4 ± 0.5 [4]. That number is therefore a prediction: a statement about what the model needs today, given what it needs at 380,000 years.

The gap is about 8–9%. Ground-based CMB experiments have since confirmed the early side. ACT DR6, combined with Planck, lensing and DESI baryon-acoustic-oscillation (BAO) data, gives 68.22 ± 0.36, or 68.43 ± 0.27 with DESI's second data release [5]. SPT-3G alone gives 66.66 ± 0.60, which is 6.2σ below the SH0ES local value, and the three CMB experiments combined give 67.19 ± 0.38 [6]. (One σ, "sigma", is one standard error; 5σ is the usual discovery bar.) BAO combined with Big Bang nucleosynthesis, a route that does not need the Planck likelihood [7], lands at 68.51 ± 0.58 [2]. A Planck-specific problem is therefore not a viable explanation, and the discrepancy persists whether or not the early-universe side leans on Planck [8].

The ladder, with a worked example

The ladder has three rungs. First, a geometric anchor, such as the nearby galaxy NGC 4258, whose absolute distance is known from a parallax-like technique, calibrates the true brightness of pulsating Cepheid stars, which follow a period–luminosity relation. Second, Cepheids in the host galaxies of Type Ia supernovae (42 supernovae in the HST sample [9]) calibrate the true peak brightness of those supernovae. Third, supernovae out in the smooth Hubble flow, far enough that peculiar motions are small, give distances from their apparent brightness, and redshifts give velocities [10][9].

Take one supernova in a galaxy receding at 7,350 km/s. If H0 = 73.5, the galaxy is at 100 Mpc. If H0 = 67.24, the CMB-based value that [2] uses, it is at 109.3 Mpc. In astronomers' units, the difference is 5 log10(73.5/67.24) ≈ 0.19 magnitudes. The SH0ES team quotes about 0.18 mag for the gap [9]. So the dispute is whether some systematic error makes every ladder distance about 0.2 mag too small, a factor of about 1.09 in distance.

Several candidate errors have been tested:

  • Cepheid crowding. Hubble Space Telescope (HST) images blend Cepheids with neighbouring stars, and the blending could grow with distance and bias the ladder. JWST resolves them. Across more than 1,000 Cepheids, JWST and HST distances differ by −0.01 ± 0.03 mag, and a crowding bias large enough to explain the tension is rejected at 8.2σ [11]. A follow-up found HST Cepheid distances agree within about 1σ with eight combinations of method, sample and telescope, with mean differences near 0.03 mag and a linearity of 0.994 ± 0.010 [9]. (TRGB, the tip of the red giant branch, and JAGB, J-region asymptotic giant branch stars, are other standard-brightness stars.)
  • Anchor or method choice. The H0DN consensus finds that removing either Cepheids or TRGB has minimal effect, and replacing supernovae with galaxy-based indicators moves H0 by less than 0.1 km/s/Mpc while doubling the error [2].
  • Supernova sample selection. This is where the two leading groups disagree most sharply.

Two teams dominate the ladder. Adam Riess's SH0ES team has measured about 73 for a decade. Wendy Freedman's Chicago-Carnegie Hubble Program (CCHP) has repeatedly landed lower. In 2024 CCHP released a JWST analysis built on three methods and a blinded process: each sub-team added a random offset to its distances and compared results only at the end. All three gave similar distances, and for a moment the tension appeared to vanish. Then the team found its Cepheid analysis had applied a crowding correction twice for about half the stars, and adding more TRGB stars to shrink that method's error bars shortened the inferred distances, which raised H0. The tension came back [10].

The published CCHP result is H0 = 70.39 ± 1.22 (stat) ± 1.33 (sys) ± 0.70 (supernova scatter) from TRGB with 24 calibrators, and JWST-only values of 68.81 (TRGB) and 67.80 (JAGB). CCHP concludes this is consistent with ΛCDM [12]. Combining the quoted errors in quadrature gives about ±1.9, which puts 70.39 only about 1.5σ above Planck (my arithmetic). Universe Today's mid-2025 summary read this as the tension possibly dissolving [13].

SH0ES's reply is that the discrepancy comes from which supernovae are used. The JWST programmes observed only about a quarter of the HST sample. The CCHP-versus-HST difference of about 2.5 km/s/Mpc is, they show, what you expect from that subsample choice. Combining all JWST samples (16 supernovae closer than 25 Mpc) gives 72.6 ± 2.0 from JWST data, against 73.2 ± 0.9 from the full HST set of 42 supernovae [9]. Riess told Quanta that CCHP's small supernova sample could give a misleading impression [10]. Freedman, for her part, doubts the Cepheid-based value because it is more sensitive to assumptions about stellar composition and dust [10].

These two accounts can both be partly right, and I found no published CCHP response to the 2026 consensus in the sources I opened. What the exchange establishes, in my reading, is that the photometry checks pass and the live question is sample selection.

2026: consensus and a ladder-free check

The H0DN "Local Distance Network" paper (A&A, April 2026) tried to settle the combinatorics. Experts voted on which indicators go into a baseline network, spanning parallaxes, eclipsing binaries, masers, Cepheids, TRGB, Mira variables, JAGB stars, Type Ia and Type II supernovae, surface-brightness fluctuations, and galaxy scaling relations. The network is weighted by the full covariance matrix, so a calibrator shared by two routes is not counted twice [2]. The result is 73.50 ± 0.81 (1.1%), with code and data released. Against the CMB-anisotropy value of 67.24 ± 0.35 (Planck+SPT+ACT), the difference is 7.1σ; against BBN+BAO it is 5.0σ [2]. Both figures are easy to check: (73.50 − 67.24)/√(0.81² + 0.35²) ≈ 7.1.

The authors conclude that explanations relying on one overlooked local error are effectively ruled out, since dropping any single method changes the answer little [3]. One caution: the author list includes SH0ES members, and the paper is an expert synthesis, not an independent re-measurement [2].

TDCOSMO 2026 addresses that gap. Lensed quasars produce multiple images whose light arrives at different times; the delays scale with H0, so the method needs no ladder [10]. The team used 11 systems, HST and JWST imaging, and stellar-motion data (JWST-NIRSpec, VLT-MUSE, Keck-KCWI) to model the lenses, and accounted for the mass-sheet degeneracy, the main modelling ambiguity. The result is 71.1 (+2.4/−2.3), a 3% measurement, which uses a supernova constraint on matter density but not the ladder [1]. Taken alone, that is only about 1.6σ above 67.4 (my arithmetic). The paper's argument is about combinations. Dropping any one of supernovae, Cepheids or TRGB leaves the tension with Planck intact. A "no-supernova" late-universe combination is 3.7σ from Planck, and after excluding the whole local ladder it is still 2.7σ [1]. This is a preprint submitted to PRL, not yet peer reviewed.

Is it really a "tension"? Competing framings and what they teach

The straightforward framing, early versus late, is not the only one. Pantos and Perivolaropoulos gathered 88 H0 measurements that do not rely on the sound horizon. The 30 distance-ladder measurements average 72.73 ± 0.39, while the other 58 average 69.37 ± 0.34, a 6.5σ gap that falls to 3.9σ after allowing for correlations [14]. They argue the disagreement separates distance-ladder measurements from everything else, and that probes assuming ΛCDM favour lower values (67.61 ± 0.96) than model-independent ones (71.03 ± 0.69), a 2.9σ internal tension [14].

That reading and the H0DN reading disagree on what counts as independent evidence. H0DN says all first-rank local indicators are internally consistent [2]. The meta-analysis mixes methods with very different assumptions and correlations, so its headline significance is dataset-dependent, a point Wang and Guo also make about the field [7]. I would call this open; a ladder-free local result at 71 is compatible with both stories (my reading).

A side note for anyone who builds evaluations: several of these patterns will be familiar. Shared calibrators create correlated errors, which is why H0DN weights by covariance rather than averaging, the same reason one judge model shared across several metrics inflates apparent agreement [2]. Blinding helped CCHP but did not prevent a double-applied correction from surfacing later [10]. Differences between subsamples, an effect Riess calls reversion to the mean, resemble the noise from small evaluation subsets [9]. And "removing any one method changes little" is a leave-one-out robustness test, which both H0DN and TDCOSMO use [2][1].

What new physics survives

If both sides are right, something in ΛCDM is incomplete. The constraint is tight. The CMB fixes the angular scale, H0 × r_s is nearly constant, and BAO distances must also fit. To raise H0 by 9% without moving the angle, r_s must shrink by roughly the same fraction (my inference from that relation). There are two levers: shorten the time sound travelled or lower its speed, in practice by changing the pre-recombination expansion or the recombination history [7][8].

  • Early dark energy (EDE). A scalar field briefly adds energy before recombination, speeding up expansion, shrinking r_s and raising H0. It is the most developed idea [7]. In the 2025 update with ACT DR6 and DESI DR2, EDE with f_EDE ≈ 0.09 ± 0.03 gives H0 = 71.0 ± 1.1 without SH0ES, the residual tension with SH0ES falls to about 2σ, and an H0 = 73 EDE fit beats ΛCDM at H0 = 68.4. ACT DR6 alone does not prefer EDE over ΛCDM [15].
  • Modified recombination or varying electron mass. SPT-3G reports a mild preference for these in a non-flat universe when CMB is combined with DESI BAO [6].
  • Late-time fixes. Changing supernova brightness is tightly constrained by inverse-distance-ladder tests, and a local void has long been ruled out as a significant contribution [8].
  • Dynamical dark energy. DESI DR2 BAO alone are in 2.3σ tension with the CMB within ΛCDM, and a time-evolving equation of state (w0, wa) is preferred at 3.1σ with CMB, or 2.8–4.2σ with supernovae depending on sample [16]. Wang and Guo caution that this is not a direct Hubble-tension fix by itself [7]. Riess includes this and a smoother-than-expected matter distribution among other hints of cracks in ΛCDM [17].

The recurring cost is that early-universe fixes shift the CMB spectra, lensing or structure growth, and may need further changes to the primordial spectrum [7][8]. A 2026 review summarises that current proposals reduce the nominal tension but at the price of correlated shifts elsewhere [7]. EDE is not dead and not confirmed. The EDE intervals above come from a profile-likelihood analysis because Bayesian posteriors without SH0ES show large prior-volume effects [15].

What to watch next

  • TDCOSMO peer review. The companion paper and peer review will test the mass-sheet treatment and the "no-ladder" 2.7σ claim [1].
  • Larger JWST calibrator samples. Both teams say more supernova hosts and anchors will settle whether sample selection is what separates 70 from 73 [10][9]. Freedman's team plans JWST observations of the Coma cluster, aimed at a route that bypasses supernovae [13].
  • CMB data. SPT-3G's 2.8σ CMB–BAO difference in ΛCDM, which extended models relax, will tighten with more data [6].
  • DESI follow-ups. The CMB-based 67 is a ΛCDM prediction [4], so if dynamical dark energy firms up, the prediction and the size of the tension both move [16][7].
  • A CCHP reply. A direct comparison against the H0DN network, on matched supernova samples, would show whether the 70.4 versus 73.5 gap is a sample effect or a method effect.

Tally. Established: three CMB experiments and BAO+BBN agree on 67–68.5 [2][5][6], and crowding errors in the Cepheid photometry are excluded [11][9]. Claimed: that the local value is 73.50 ± 0.81 and robust to dropping any single method [2][1], that EDE-like physics closes the gap [15], and that the tension may be dissolving [12][13]. Open: supernova sample selection, the gap between CCHP and H0DN, and whether the tension is nearer 4σ or 7σ depending on what you count [14].
