arXiv: Oct 8 · 10m
LLM judges flip verdicts on reruns and answer-order swaps; one fully repeatable judge was right only 51% of the time  —  A reliability audit of LLM-as-a-judge runs six frontier models across four benchmarks, five prompt formats, two presentation orders, three temperatures and ten repeats. Verdicts change between identical runs even at temperature zero, swapping answer order flips most verdicts on hard tasks, and one perfectly repeatable judge matched ground truth only 51% of the time. The authors propose a trustworthy verdict rate and find holistic rubric scoring beats pairwise win-rate prompting.
Takeaway: Directly relevant to your LLM-as-judge reliability work: it separates determinism from accuracy and offers a metric combining reproducibility, order-invariance and correctness that you could add to judge checks.

Arize AI blog: Oct 7 · 8m
Reversing a question's wording flipped several small judge models from good to worse than chance  —  Arize benchmarked eight small decision models (classifiers that return a probability per option) as judges for hallucination detection, evaluator suites and agent routing. Accuracy among the leaders was within about a point. The notable result: rewording the RAGTruth question to reverse its polarity on 1,338 rows dropped Liquid d1 from 0.92 to 0.17 ROC AUC and Strands Decider from 0.75 to 0.26, while Jev held at 0.93. This is a vendor-run benchmark.
Takeaway: Bears on your judge-reliability checks: swapping the judge model can silently invert scores, so testing both phrasings of each rubric question is a cheap guard to add to evaluation pipelines.
