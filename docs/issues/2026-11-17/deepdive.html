Long-Horizon Agent Reliability: Context Engineering, Trajectory Verification, and Harness Design

Why multi-step LLM agents compound errors over extended runs—and how compaction, trajectory-level judging, and decoupled harnesses make them dependable.

Executive Takeaways

  • Error compounding and context rot keep multi-step reliability flat: across 15 models from 2024–2026 on GAIA and τ-bench, capability gains yielded little improvement in consistency or predictability.
  • Context engineering treats attention as a finite budget—pairing restorable filesystem offloading, immutable KV-cache prefixes (~10x cached token savings), and external session logs over fragile in-place compaction.
  • Outcome-only LLM judges fail silently: on 400 mutated trajectories, a 14B outcome judge scored 34%–76% apparent recall with zero paired discrimination on reply-unchanged faults, whereas step-by-step checks reached +1.00.
  • Modern agent harnesses decouple the reasoning loop from sandboxed execution and move credentials into edge proxies, because default coding-agent sandboxes still expose readable secrets and unsandboxed MCP servers.
  • Automated harness search is not magic: under matched inference budgets, harness evolution failed to beat simple test-time scaling on Terminal-Bench 2.1, winning mainly on out-of-distribution long-horizon games.
Strategic Context: Directly connects your focus on LLM agent evaluation, LLM-as-judge reliability, prompt optimization, and runtime/harness engineering to the past year's sharpest production architectures and empirical audits.

In the span of three weeks this autumn, three separate disclosures punctured the assumption that stronger foundation models automatically yield dependable autonomous agents. On November 3, a NeurIPS 2026 workshop camera-ready study (trajectory-judge) showed that an outcome-only LLM judge reading only a user request and final reply can look impressive on raw recall—flagging 34% to 76% of injected faults—while achieving literally zero paired discrimination over clean runs [11]. Days earlier, on October 29, threat researchers detailed a clandestine shadow AI relay that hijacked OpenAI Codex and Anthropic Claude Code installations at a dozen enterprises by disabling their local sandboxes and swapping in relay-issued tokens [17], echoing an October 5 audit showing that even when vendor sandboxes work as documented, credentials still reach agent commands across all five major coding CLIs [16]. And on October 5, researchers from the Allen Institute for AI and the University of Washington revised an evaluation audit demonstrating that automatic "harness evolution" loops—touted across 2026 as a way to optimize agent scaffolds automatically—often fail to outperform simple matched-budget test-time sampling on Terminal-Bench 2.1 [18].

Together, these findings mark a turning point for production agent engineering. Frontier models can now write thousands of lines of code, navigate live browsers, and sustain coherent tool loops for hours [5][8]. Yet as execution horizons stretch from five steps to fifty or five hundred, the binding constraint has shifted from raw model intelligence to systems reliability: how context is curated, how intermediate actions are sandboxed and verified, and how the runtime harness governs failure.

Why Multi-Step Agents Compound Failure

In single-turn chat, a 98% per-step reliability rate looks almost solved. Over a 50-step trajectory—roughly the average length of a production task in Manus [4]—independent errors multiply to 0.98^50 ≈ 0.36, leaving nearly two-thirds of runs broken even before accounting for correlated state corruption. Once an agent misreads a file path in step four or skips an eligibility check in step nine, every downstream step conditions on a poisoned trace.

Empirical benchmarks confirm that higher single-step accuracy has not closed this reliability gap. In Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability (revised June 2026 for ICML 2026), Princeton researchers evaluated 15 models released between early 2024 and mid-2026—spanning OpenAI's GPT-4 Turbo through GPT-5.5, Google's Gemini 2.5 through 3.5 Flash, and Anthropic's Claude 3 Haiku through Claude Opus 4.7—across GAIA's 165 validation tasks and a verified 26-task airline subset of τ-bench (filtered after prior work found flawed labels or ambiguous specs in 24 of the original 50 airline tasks) [1]. Decomposing reliability into 12 accuracy-independent metrics across consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety, the authors found that 24 months of capability leaps produced only modest reliability gains [1]. Newer models solved harder tasks on average, yet remained surprisingly inconsistent across repeated runs, brittle to prompt rephrasings, and poorly calibrated about when they were about to fail [1].

Part of that fragility is baked into how transformers consume growing histories. A context window—the full set of tokens fed into an LLM at each sampling step—is often marketed as if million-token capacity implies uniform recall [2][3]. In July 2025, Chroma tested 18 leading closed and open-weight models (including GPT-4.1, Claude 4, Gemini 2.5, and Qwen3) while holding task complexity fixed and varying only input length [2]. Even on simple text replication and semantic retrieval tasks, performance degraded non-uniformly as token count grew, a phenomenon dubbed context rot [2]. Degradation accelerated when the needle-to-question semantic similarity dropped, when topically related distractors were inserted, or when a model had to locate relevant history inside a 113K-token LongMemEval transcript before reasoning over it [2].

Anthropic frames this constraint as a finite attention budget [3]. Because self-attention computes n^2 pairwise relationships across n tokens—and models encounter far more short sequences than ultra-long ones during training—every noisy stack trace, stale file dump, or unused tool schema dilutes attention across the rest of the prompt [3]. Adding a million tokens of context capacity does not eliminate context pollution; it merely delays an out-of-context crash while quietly eroding reasoning precision along the way [2][3].

Context Engineering: Compaction, Offloading, and the KV-Cache

If context is a depreciating attention budget, context engineering is the discipline of dynamically curating the smallest high-signal token stream at each step of an agent loop [3][4]. Where prompt engineering tuned static system instructions for one-shot tasks, context engineering manages the entire evolving runtime state: system rules, tool schemas, external files, and message histories [3].

Production teams have converged on four core context tactics, though they disagree sharply on how much isolation to introduce:

  • Progressive disclosure and external filesystems. Rather than stuffing an entire codebase or knowledge base into the system prompt, OpenAI's Codex engineering team—who shipped roughly one million lines of code across 1,500 pull requests with zero manually written code over five months—found that a giant AGENTS.md manual "rots instantly" and crowds out task constraints [5]. They shrank AGENTS.md to a roughly 100-line table of contents pointing into a structured docs/ tree verified by CI linters and a recurring "doc-gardening" agent [5]. Both Manus and Anthropic use the sandbox filesystem the same way: dumping raw web scrapes, build logs, and search results onto disk while keeping only lightweight pointers (file paths, URLs, head/tail snippets) in active context so discarded observations remain restorable [3][4][9].
  • Compaction versus hard context resets. Compaction summarizes older turns in place when the window nears capacity so the same trajectory can continue [3][8]. Yet in November 2025, Anthropic reported that compaction alone was insufficient for multi-hour builds: Claude Sonnet 4.5 suffered from "context anxiety," wrapping up work prematurely as it sensed its window filling [8][14]. Anthropic paired an initializer agent (creating init.sh, a claude-progress.txt log, and a JSON feature_list.json of over 200 failing requirements) with incremental coding sessions that ran hard context resets—wiping the context window clean and handing off state via structured files and git history [7][8]. Tellingly, when Anthropic upgraded to Opus 4.5 and Opus 4.6, context anxiety vanished; the resets became dead weight and were removed [8][14].
  • KV-cache stability and attention recitation. In an agent loop, each step appends a short tool call and a large environment observation; Manus reports an average input-to-output token ratio near 100:1 [4]. Because cached input tokens on frontier APIs can cost 10x less than uncached prefill ($0.30 versus $3.00 per million tokens on Claude Sonnet in Manus's accounting), cache hit rate dominates both cost and time-to-first-token (TTFT) [4]. Manus keeps prompt prefixes immutable—banning per-second timestamps at the top of system prompts, enforcing deterministic JSON key ordering, and using token-logit masking instead of adding or removing tool schemas mid-loop, which invalidates prefixes and confuses the model [4]. To combat goal drift across 50+ tool calls without breaking the prefix, Manus rewrites a todo.md checklist at the tail of the trace, constantly reciting global objectives into recent attention [4].
  • Keeping failures in the trace. While it is tempting to scrub failed tool calls and retry silently, Manus found that leaving failed actions and stack traces in context helps the model update its priors and avoid repeating dead ends, whereas overly uniform action-observation history induces few-shot mimicry ruts [4].

Here, however, major builders diverge. In Don't Build Multi-Agents (June 2025), Cognition argued that splitting a long task across parallel subagents breaks reliability because actions carry implicit design assumptions: two subagents building a game background and character sprite in isolation produce incompatible artifacts that a coordinator cannot reconcile [6]. Cognition favors a single-threaded continuous trace backed by a dedicated compression model [6]. Anthropic and LangChain take the opposite stance for context-heavy tasks: LangChain's Deep Agents and Anthropic's research architectures deliberately spawn isolated subagents for read-heavy exploration or independent QA precisely so sprawling search traces do not pollute the main orchestrator's attention budget [3][8][9]. What is established is that uncoordinated parallel writers fail unless they share an explicit state contract on disk [6][7]; what remains open is how much cross-agent context passing frontier models can sustain before coordination overhead outweighs context savings [6][9].

A Worked Example: Anatomy of a 50-Step Trajectory Failure

To see how context rot, error compounding, and evaluation blind spots interact in practice, trace a realistic 50-step task: a customer-support or code-maintenance agent asked to audit open refund requests or migrate a billing module.

Suppose in step 4 the agent queries an order database and runs a test script whose stdout returns 45,000 tokens of unfiltered JSON and stack logs. In a naive harness, those raw tokens enter the prompt verbatim, immediately pushing earlier policy rules into the middle of the window where recall sags [2][3]. By step 12, facing ambiguous distractors from earlier orders, the agent skips a mandatory check_eligibility tool call (or misreads a schema constraint) and calls issue_refund for the exact correct euro amount [11].

In step 18, the environment returns a clean HTTP 200 because the refund API itself does not enforce the support desk's standard operating procedure [11]. If the harness uses silent retry-and-scrub logic on minor formatting errors, no evidence of early hesitation survives in the trace [4]. By step 48, the agent writes a polite, factually accurate final message: "I have refunded EUR 398.50 for order ORD-20000" [11].

Now watch what happens under three different architectures:

  1. Naive loop plus outcome-only LLM judge. The reply looks identical to a compliant run [11]. An outcome judge given only the user request and final reply sees nothing wrong—or flags randomly based on its base skepticism—yielding zero net discrimination between the policy violation and a clean parent trajectory [11].
  2. Naive compaction. If a summarizer compressed steps 1–20 at step 25 into "Checked order ORD-20000 and processed refund," the missing precondition is permanently erased from recoverable history, making post-hoc debugging impossible [3][14].
  3. Engineered harness plus step-level verification. A Manus- or Managed-Agents-style harness offloads the 45,000-token log to a sandbox file (retaining only a pointer and tail summary) [4][9], logs every raw action in an immutable external session stream (emitEvent) [14], blocks unapproved egress or credential reads at the OS/proxy boundary [15][16], and runs deterministic precondition assertions alongside a step-aware verifier that checks each tool call against policy before certifying completion [11][13].

The difference is not a smarter base model; it is an execution and verification loop designed around the assumption that single steps will quietly go wrong.

Trajectory Verification: Beyond Outcome-Only Judges

As agents move from chat into stateful tools, evaluating whether a long run succeeded has become almost as hard as executing it. Rule-based environment checks—asserting exact final database fields or DOM states—were long treated as the gold standard, yet they brittlely reject valid alternative paths.

In AgentRewardBench (revised October 2025), researchers from McGill, Mila, Google DeepMind, and ServiceNow collected 1,302 web-agent trajectories across five benchmarks (including WebArena, VisualWebArena, and WorkArena++) and four LLM agents, annotated by human experts for task success, unintended side effects, and repetition loops [10]. Testing 12 LLM judges alongside official rule-based evaluators, they found that rule-based metrics systematically underreported agent capability—underestimating GPT-4o's success rate by 16.7 percentage points on WebArena and 18.5 percentage points on VisualWebArena—and even inverted model rankings relative to expert judgments [10]. Yet LLM judges had the opposite bias: they tended to overestimate success, misled by grounding mismatches (believing an agent's chain-of-thought claim about what appeared on screen without checking screenshots) and missed instruction constraints (crediting an agent that put cereal in a cart when asked to buy it) [10].

The November 2026 trajectory-judge study (2609.00038) exposes an even sharper blind spot when judges inspect outcomes rather than full traces [11]. Using a deterministic support-desk testbed with a scripted oracle and single-step fault injector across 400 exactly labeled trajectories, Hadi Mohammadi tested a 14B judge on six fault types, pairing every faulty run with the clean parent trajectory it was mutated from [11]. On four fault types that leave the final reply byte-identical to the clean run (such as skipping a required eligibility check before issuing a valid refund), an outcome-only judge shown only the request and reply scored 34% to 76% raw recall—a number that looks like partial detection until paired against clean runs reveals zero paired discrimination (Δ = 0) [11]. Even splitting results by whether the fault broke the environment state ("loud" versus "silent" faults) failed to rescue outcome judging: 55 of the judge's 105 flags on loud faults came from reply-unchanged mutations flagged at the exact same rate as clean parents, reducing an apparent 84% loud recall to +0.393 paired discrimination [11].

When the same 14B model was shown every intermediate step and instructed to verify each one, paired discrimination jumped to +1.00 on those four fault types with 0 false positives across 100 clean runs [11]. Crucially, however, it still failed to reliably verify claims inside the final reply: across four types of invented promises, it caught one consistently and missed the other three in 41 of 42 faults [11]. Meanwhile, an 8B judge showed no useful discrimination under the same prompt, and majority voting over three samples added cost without measurable gain [11].

To scale step-level evaluation without drowning in token costs, researchers are turning to Process Reward Models (PRMs) built specifically for interactive trajectories. Unlike math reasoning chains where each equation is locally right or wrong, agent actions often look ambiguous until several turns later. AgentPRM (November 2025) scores intermediate agent actions by step-wise promise and progress toward the goal, using Temporal Difference learning with Generalized Advantage Estimation to label trajectories automatically [12]. Across interactive browser and shopping benchmarks, AgentPRM achieved over 8x compute efficiency compared to outcome-supervised baselines when scaling test-time search [12]. Still, limitations remain clear: Mohammadi's paired-discrimination audit tested synthetic single-fault injections on two local models rather than messy multi-fault frontier deployments [11], and both PRMs and LLM judges remain vulnerable to ungrounded reasoning traces when environment observations are incomplete [10][12].

Harness Engineering: Decoupling Brains, Hands, and Boundaries

By 2026, industry labs and framework authors converged on a succinct equation: Agent = Model + Harness [9][13]. An agent harness encompasses everything around the model weights: system prompts, tool schemas, filesystem and browser sandboxes, compaction middleware, orchestration loops, and deterministic verification hooks [9].

In April 2026, Thoughtworks Distinguished Engineer Birgitta Böckeler framed the user-facing outer harness as a cybernetic governor built from feedforward guides (steering the agent before it acts, via AGENTS.md, skills, or project templates) and feedback sensors (observing outputs after it acts so the agent can self-correct) [13]. Crucially, sensors split into computational controls—type checkers, custom linters, and architectural fitness tests that run in milliseconds and return deterministic remediation messages—and inferential controls, such as LLM code reviewers or browser QA agents that are slower, costlier, and probabilistic [13]. OpenAI's Codex team pushed computational enforcement to the limit: their harness mechanically enforces a strict six-layer domain dependency graph (Types -> Config -> Repo -> Service -> Runtime -> UI, with cross-cutting concerns entering only through Providers), runs an ephemeral local observability stack per git worktree queryable via LogQL and PromQL, and wires Chrome DevTools Protocol into the runtime so Codex can validate UI state itself during six-hour runs [5].

Anthropic's March 2026 experiments on full-stack app generation illustrate both the power and the price of inferential harnesses [8]. Inspired by Generative Adversarial Networks, Anthropic paired a Planner (expanding a one-sentence prompt into a feature spec), a Generator, and a Playwright-equipped Evaluator that negotiated testable "sprint contracts" and clicked through live builds—because models asked to grade their own work routinely praised broken software [8]. On a 2D retro game-maker prompt using Claude Opus 4.5, a solo 20-minute ($9) run produced broken entity wiring where gameplay failed completely, whereas the three-agent harness ran for 6 hours ($200) and delivered a playable game [8]. Upgrading to Opus 4.6 let Anthropic strip out the sprint decomposition while keeping the planner and final QA passes: building a browser Digital Audio Workstation took 3 hours 50 minutes and $124.70, with the standalone QA agent still catching stubbed recording buttons and missing drag-and-drop handlers that the generator left behind [8].

To make long-horizon harnesses scale reliably in production, Anthropic subsequently re-architected Claude Managed Agents (April 2026) around an OS-style virtualization boundary: decoupling the brain (model plus stateless harness) from the hands (containers accessed strictly via an execute(name, input) -> string tool interface) and the session (a durable, append-only external event log queried via getEvents()) [14]. In their original single-container setup, every crashed container killed the session ("pets"), forced customers to peer VPC networks, and stalled inference while repositories cloned up front [14]. Treating containers and harnesses as stateless "cattle" allowed inference to begin before provisioning sandboxes—cutting p50 TTFT by roughly 60% and p95 TTFT by over 90%—while keeping context recoverable outside any destructive compaction window [14].

That decoupling also proved essential for sandboxing, where 2026 revealed painful gaps between marketing and reality. Anthropic's October 2025 OS-level sandboxing for Claude Code (using Linux bubblewrap and macOS Seatbelt to enforce both filesystem write restrictions and proxy-mediated network allowlists) reduced interactive permission prompts by 84% [15]. Yet when security firm Riptides tested five major coding agent sandboxes—Claude Code, Codex CLI, Gemini CLI, GitHub Copilot CLI, and Cursor—on September 30, 2026, it found a shared Achilles' heel: sandboxes tightly lock down writes, while four of five allow broad disk reads by default, and all five still let credentials reach sandboxed commands via readable files, inherited *TOKEN* environment variables, or deliberate GH_TOKEN exports [16]. Moreover, in three of the five tools, MCP servers ran outside the sandbox with full user privileges [16]. On Windows, Riptides observed Codex's unelevated network block failing to stop Node from reaching api.github.com and Copilot silently disabling its sandbox on Windows Server 2025 with a warning [16]. Weeks later, Aviatrix and Symantec documented an active supply-chain campaign in which lookalike install scripts hijacked Codex and Claude Code CLIs at a dozen enterprises, turning off sandboxes and routing prompts and secrets through attacker-controlled relays [17]. Because a prompt-injected agent only needs to read a secret once to leak it into its next model request or allowed domain, both Anthropic's Managed Agents and Riptides conclude that credentials must live outside the sandbox in an authenticating proxy or vault that injects auth at the network edge [14][15][16].

Finally, rigorous academic scrutiny is tempering the rush toward fully automated harness optimization. In Harnesses for Inference-Time Alignment over Execution Trajectories (May 2026), researchers formalized harnesses into task decomposition and guided execution, proving mathematically and empirically that over-decomposition and over-pruning can actually lower pass rates—and that partial harnesses specifying only initial steps often beat rigid end-to-end workflows [19]. Five months later, Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution for Agents (2607.12227, revised October 2026) audited automated harness-search loops under matched feedback and inference budgets with strict train/test splits [18]. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, evolved harnesses failed to beat simple parallel sampling or sequential refinement and generalized poorly to held-out tasks [18]. Only in out-of-distribution, long-horizon games with granular feedback did automated harness evolution deliver genuine lift—improving over matched-budget sequential refinement by 80% on ARC-AGI-3 and 11% on EdgeBench [18].

What to Watch Next

Over the next two quarters, four engineering signals will separate durable agent platforms from brittle demos:

  • Matched-budget, held-out harness benchmarks. Expect evaluations to adopt 2607.12227's discipline—comparing prompt and harness optimizers against equal-compute parallel sampling on strictly held-out tasks—before declaring scaffold wins [18].
  • Paired trajectory judge audits in observability stacks. As teams deploy LLM-as-judge monitors in production, watch for trajectory-judge-style paired mutation suites and step-level PRMs replacing naive outcome-only pass rates [11][12].
  • Vault-backed credential proxies and microVM isolation. Look for coding agents and MCP runtimes to move credentials out of environment variables and local dotfiles into network-edge proxies and dedicated VMs, closing the read-path leak exposed in late 2026 [14][16][17].
  • Model absorption of harness scaffolding. Every harness rule encodes a hypothesis about what current models fail to do alone [8][14]. Watching which today-essential scaffolds—like compaction heuristics, tool-search discovery, or sprint contracts—vanish with the next wave of post-trained frontier weights will tell builders where to invest engineering complexity next [8][9][14].
