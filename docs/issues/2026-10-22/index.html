Menthi Kura Guddu Vepudu (Fenugreek-Leaf Egg Fry)మెంతికూర గుడ్డు వేపుడు

Fresh fenugreek leaves are wilted hard in a garlicky talimpu so the bitterness turns into a savoury edge, then four eggs are set in the greens until they cling in golden, spicy curds. Fifteen minutes, one pan, and nothing like a plain scramble.

  • 15 g protein
  • 17 g fat
  • 244 kcal

Ingredients
  • fresh fenugreek leaves (menthi kura), picked and finely choppedabout 2 packed cups · 80 g
  • eggs, shelled4 large · 200 g
  • ghee1 tbsp · 14 g
  • cumin seeds1/2 tsp · 1.5 g
  • garlic, lightly crushed3 cloves · 6 g
  • dried red chillies, as Guntur karam2 chillies or 1 tsp · 2 g
  • curry leaves10 leaves · 1.5 g
  • onion, finely chopped2 tbsp · 30 g
  • green chilli, slit2 chillies · 8 g
  • turmeric1/4 tsp · 1 g
  • saltabout 1/2 tsp · 2.5 g
Method
  1. Pick the fenugreek leaves from the stems, wash and drain well, then chop fine. Beat the eggs with half the salt and a pinch of turmeric.
  2. Heat the ghee in a kadai over medium heat. Add cumin; when it sizzles and darkens (about 10 seconds), add the garlic, dried chillies and curry leaves and fry until the garlic turns pale gold and the leaves stop spluttering, about 40 seconds.
  3. Add the onion and green chilli and fry 2 minutes until translucent with brown edges.
  4. Add the fenugreek leaves, remaining turmeric and salt. Toss on medium-high for 2 to 3 minutes until the leaves collapse and the pan looks nearly dry. This is what removes the raw bitterness.
  5. Reduce to medium, pour in the eggs and let them sit 20 seconds, then fold gently every 15 seconds for 2 to 3 minutes. Cook until no liquid egg remains and the curds are firm (160 F / 71 C); stop there so they stay tender.
  6. Rest 1 minute and serve hot, with a squeeze of lemon if you like.
Make it taste like home
  • Salting the chopped leaves for 5 minutes and squeezing them softens bitterness further, but costs some flavour; the hard wilt in step 4 is usually enough.
  • Keto: a measured 30 g of onion keeps this breakfast low; no tomato here, so the dish stays dry and fast.
  • Make ahead: the fenugreek-onion base (steps 2 to 4) can be cooked the night before and refrigerated in a covered container up to 2 days. Reheat to steaming and add the eggs fresh. Cooked egg dishes keep refrigerated for 3 to 4 days and must be reheated until steaming hot (165 F / 74 C).
Swaps
  • Mild: use one green chilli and 1/2 tsp chilli powder.
  • Dairy-free: use sesame or groundnut oil instead of ghee.

Method
  1. Toss the goat with yogurt, half the garlic and ginger, turmeric, half the chilli powder, salt and 1/2 cup water straight in the cooker pot; no marinating time is needed with small cubes.
  2. Pressure-cook on high for 15 minutes (about 4 to 5 whistles on a stovetop cooker), then release pressure and open. The meat should yield easily when pressed with a fork; if not, reseal and cook 5 to 8 more minutes. Whole cuts of goat are safe from 145 F / 63 C but need this long cooking to turn tender.
  3. Meanwhile mix the coriander powder, fennel, pepper and garam masala. Set the cooker back on the heat uncovered, and boil off the liquid for 3 to 4 minutes until only a few spoonfuls of thick gravy remain.
  4. In a kadai heat the ghee and oil over medium-high. Add curry leaves and green chilli and fry until the leaves stop spluttering, about 30 seconds. Add the onion and remaining garlic and ginger; fry 3 minutes until golden at the edges.
  5. Add the meat and its gravy and the remaining chilli powder. Fry on high, scraping, 5 to 6 minutes until the oil separates and the masala coats the meat and darkens. Add the spice mix in the last 2 minutes so the pepper does not burn.
  6. Finish with lemon juice and coriander, and rest 2 minutes before serving.
Make it taste like home
  • Taste tip: crush pepper and fennel just before using; the dish depends on pepper heat as much as chilli.
  • Keto: onion is the only meaningful carb at 40 g for the pot; do not add tomato or tamarind.
  • Make ahead: the cooked meat can be pressure-cooked in advance, cooled within 2 hours and refrigerated up to 3 days; fry just before serving. Reheat fully to steaming hot (165 F / 74 C) and freeze portions up to 2 weeks.
  • Time check: about 40 minutes in total once pressure build-up and release are counted; it is a good weekend or batch-cook dish.
Swaps
  • Mild: halve the chilli powder and use one green chilli.
  • Bone-in goat needs 25 to 30 minutes under pressure, so keep to boneless for weekdays.

Method
  1. Pat the squid very dry with paper towels, then toss with turmeric, half the pepper and the salt. Dry squid fries instead of stewing.
  2. Heat the coconut oil in a kadai on high until it shimmers. Add mustard seeds; when they pop, add dried chillies and curry leaves and fry 20 seconds until the leaves stop spluttering.

  • Keto: squid carries about 3 g carb per 100 g, which is counted; no tamarind used (the Kerala-style sources add a little).
  • Storage: eat fresh. If keeping leftovers, refrigerate within 2 hours and use within 2 days; reheat quickly to steaming hot (165 F / 74 C), and expect firmer squid.

  • Mild: use one green chilli and omit the dried chillies.
  • Frozen cleaned squid rings work: thaw, then pat very dry.

Ingredient Dossier

Menthi kura (fresh fenugreek leaves)

Menthi kura is the quiet workhorse of Andhra kitchens: small, clover-like leaves with a bitter, almost maple-and-celery perfume that blooms in hot fat. Bitterness is the point, and the technique is to tame it rather than hide it. Andhra cooks do it three ways: a hard wilt in talimpu (cumin, garlic, dried chilli, curry leaves) until the pan is nearly dry; a sour counterweight such as tomato or a touch of tamarind in pappu; or grinding the leaves into a coarse roti pachadi with dried chillies. Today's breakfast uses the first approach and folds eggs into the leaves, so you taste green, garlicky heat rather than a plain scramble.

For keto, the leaves are a good deal: about 6 g carbohydrate and 1 g fibre per 100 g by the IFCT figure in our cache, and a recipe needs only a handful, so 80 g across two servings counts as roughly 2 g net per plate. Skip the toor dal that menthi kura pappu usually carries; it is the real carb in that dish. Pick the leaves from the stems (stems are the bitterest part), wash in two changes of water and drain thoroughly, because wet leaves steam instead of fry.

Use it in egg fries, with paneer, folded into keema in the last two minutes, or ground into a pachadi with sesame oil. The leaves keep 3 to 4 days in the fridge wrapped in a dry towel inside a bag.

Where to find itFresh bunches at Indian grocery stores such as Patel Brothers (Santa Clara) or India Cash & Carry (San Jose, Fremont) (often by the spinach); frozen chopped methi is a fine substitute (squeeze it dry). Kasuri methi is a different, dried product.

Keto noteAbout 6 g carbohydrate (1 g fibre) per 100 g, so a typical 40 g serving adds roughly 2 g net carbs.

How far can you trust an LLM judge?

Position bias, rerun variance and the kappa gap: what 2024 to 2026 studies say about judges for agent evaluation.

Executive Analytical Takeaways

  • Raw agreement overstates judge quality: chance-corrected kappa was 33 to 41 points lower on MT-Bench across 21 judges [1].
  • A judge can be highly repeatable (test-retest above 0.95) and still strongly position-biased, so rerun checks alone prove little [1].
  • Judge upgrades are not interchangeable, and jury averaging adds little when errors are correlated [2].
  • Name the human annotator pool, run both answer orders, report rerun variance, and revalidate on every change.
Strategic Relevance

Your agent-evaluation work depends on judges being valid, not merely repeatable, and this maps which checks to run before trusting one.

Last Friday, Kili Technology published a data story that pulls two summer 2026 preprints into one uncomfortable claim: an LLM judge can pass every consistency check you would think to run and still disagree with the experts it replaces [9]. The timing matters. Judges now gate releases, rank checkpoints and score agent trajectories, and the evidence from the last eighteen months has moved from "judges have biases" to "the standard validation numbers are the wrong ones." This piece walks through what is established, what rests on single studies, and what a small agent-evaluation team can do on Monday.

What the judge is, and what "trust" means

An LLM-as-a-judge is a model prompted to score or compare outputs, either pointwise (a grade against a rubric) or pairwise (which of two answers is better). A broad survey frames the central question as how to build a reliable judge system, and splits the answer into consistency, bias mitigation and adaptation to the task [7]. That split is useful, because "trust" bundles four properties that fail independently:

  • Validity: do the judge's verdicts match what a competent human expert would conclude?
  • Test-retest reliability: does the same judge give the same verdict when you ask again?
  • Bias robustness: does the verdict change for reasons unrelated to quality, such as answer order, length or authorship?
  • Reference quality: are the human labels you validate against themselves trustworthy?

Most teams check one or two of these. The recent studies are about the gaps between them.

Known biases: position, verbosity, self-preference

Position bias is a preference for an answer because of where it sits in the prompt. A systematic study of 15 judges on MT-Bench and DevBench (22 tasks, roughly 40 answer-generating models, over 150,000 evaluation instances) found the bias is not random chance and varies strongly across judges and tasks [4]. It is only weakly affected by prompt length but strongly affected by the quality gap between the two answers [4]. That last point has a practical reading: position bias bites hardest on close calls, which are exactly the comparisons you run a judge to resolve. The authors also separate three measurements: repetition stability (same verdict on reruns), position consistency (same winner after swapping order) and preference fairness (whether one slot is favoured overall) [4].

Verbosity bias, a preference for longer answers, is the folklore bias. The evidence is more mixed than the folklore. A 2026 audit of 21 judges measured verbosity bias below 0.011 across its cohort, but under a single pairwise rubric, so the authors do not claim it vanishes elsewhere [1]. A separate July 2026 study found that stronger judges reduce position and verbosity bias but do not remove either [2]. The honest summary: verbosity bias is probe-dependent. Measure it on your own tasks instead of assuming it is large or negligible.

Self-preference is a judge scoring its own outputs above others of equal human-rated quality. One explanation is self-recognition: models can distinguish their own text from other models' and humans' text out of the box, and after fine-tuning the strength of self-recognition correlated linearly with the strength of self-preference [6]. A competing, complementary account introduces a quantitative self-preference metric, finds a significant effect for GPT-4, and argues the root is familiarity: judges give higher scores to lower-perplexity text than humans do, whether or not the judge wrote it [5]. These are not the same claim. One says "I recognise myself"; the other says "I like what is statistically easy for me." For an agent team the operational consequence is the same: do not let a model family grade its own agent traces without a cross-family check.

Why agreement percentages mislead: the kappa gap

The sharpest recent result concerns how judges are validated. A June 2026 preprint ran 21 judges from nine providers on MT-Bench, JudgeBench and RewardBench under three protocols (agreement, consistency, bias audit), across 118 runs and about 541,000 judgments [1]. Its first finding is "kappa deflation": switching from exact-match agreement to Cohen's kappa, which corrects for chance agreement, lowers measured quality for every judge, by 33 to 41 percentage points on MT-Bench [1].

A worked example makes the mechanism concrete. Suppose 90% of your items are ties and a judge always answers "tie." Its raw agreement with human labels is 90%; its kappa is zero, because it carries no information beyond the label frequencies. Real judges are less extreme, but the same arithmetic applies. As reported by Kili, the judge with the smallest gap on MT-Bench matched the expert label 84.9% of the time with kappa 0.511, while GPT-4o-mini matched 80.9% with kappa 0.396 [9]. Those figures come from the secondary write-up of the paper; check the paper's tables before quoting them elsewhere.

The same study found that rankings of judges are unstable across benchmarks, with ranks shifting by up to 14 positions [1]. Picking a judge because it topped one benchmark is picking an instrument by the wrong criterion.

Consistent and wrong: the reliability-validity gap

The most useful finding for anyone who validates judges by rerunning them is the "consistency-bias paradox." Two production-deployed judges in the 2026 audit showed test-retest reliability above 0.95 while also showing position bias above 0.10 [1]. Kili's write-up names them as Qwen 3 8B and Gemini 2.5 Flash, with position-bias scores of 0.192 and 0.125 on MT-Bench [9]. The score measures how far the preference for the first-shown answer drifts from an even split. A judge that gives the same biased answer every time passes a rerun test perfectly.

An independent line of evidence in a different domain points the same way. A fixed-benchmark study compared four LLM evaluators with aggregated ratings from 15 counseling experts on 18 simulated Japanese-language counseling sessions, each judge scoring each transcript three times [3]. Single-run intraclass correlation coefficients ranged from .33 to .96, and high run-to-run reliability did not ensure closer alignment with the experts; all four systems also scored higher than the experts on two constructs [3]. The study's own caveat is important: one session per condition-by-profile cell means conclusions apply to those sessions, not every possible regeneration [3]. It is a preprint (first posted June 2025, with a major revision in August 2026), so treat it as a supporting data point, not a settled law.

Rerun noise is also a measurement problem in its own right. A 2026 workshop study of design choices found that evaluation criteria were critical for reliability, that non-deterministic sampling improved alignment with human preferences relative to deterministic decoding, and that chain-of-thought reasoning gave minimal gains once clear criteria were present [8]. This runs against two common defaults: temperature zero and "think step by step" are not free wins. It is one study on two benchmarks, so test it on your task rather than flipping the default.

Swapping the judge, and the problem with the gold labels

Teams upgrade judges as models improve. A July 2026 study treats this as a measurement-validity problem: across four datasets, only one Qwen3 step (1.7B to 4B) gave a robust adjacent gain, MiniMax adjacent releases did not, and the authors conclude judge upgrades are not interchangeable [2]. The same paper reports that repeated-sample juries add little when judge errors are correlated, which is the quiet failure of "just average five samples" [2]. It also notes that structured debate can shift decisions substantially, but without parser and fallback logs those shifts cannot be attributed to deliberation [2]. The recommendation is to report dataset slices, bias probes, error-dependence estimates and protocol audit trails [2]. Every judge change is a new instrument and needs revalidating.

Then there is the reference side. According to Kili's summary of an August 2026 annotator-pool audit, expert and crowd majorities differed on 23.6% of items where both groups were internally unanimous, and opposite winners were named on 9.2%; three open-weight judges agreed more with the crowd than with experts [9]. The same summary reports that six-model leaderboards were identical under either pool, but that the chance of at least one displaced model rises to 0.86 with ten models and 0.9997 with twenty [9]. I could not open the primary paper for that audit, so this block is a secondary claim, and the judges tested were mid-size open models. The directional lesson is that "agreement with humans" is underspecified until you name which humans.

For agent evaluation specifically, Kili also relays a June preprint that audited 496 expert-reviewed tool-calling tasks and found the automated evaluator disagreed with experts on 92 (18.5%), and that 23 reruns of one benchmark setup spanned 57.9% to 76.8% [9]. Again secondary, but it is the right order of magnitude to keep in mind: when run-to-run spread is nearly 19 points, a three-point gap between two agents is not a result.

What is established, claimed and open

Established (multiple independent studies): position bias is real, varies by judge and task, and grows when answers are close in quality [4]; self-preference exists and is tied to familiarity or self-recognition [5][6]; stronger judges reduce but do not eliminate bias [2]; raw agreement overstates judge quality relative to chance-corrected measures [1].

Claimed, single-study or secondary: the consistency-bias paradox in specific named judges [1][9]; sampling beating deterministic decoding and CoT adding little [8]; the expert-versus-crowd split and leaderboard fragility [9]; the counseling-domain gap between reliability and alignment [3].

Open: whether verbosity bias is genuinely small or only under one rubric [1]; how any of this transfers to multi-step agent trajectories, where the judge reads long, tool-laden transcripts that most of these benchmarks do not resemble; and what a defensible human reference is when experts themselves disagree.

A practical protocol for an agent-evaluation team

  1. Build an expert-labelled calibration set and name the annotator pool. Keep per-annotator labels instead of collapsing to a majority vote, so you can stratify later.
  2. Measure the humans first. Compute chance-corrected inter-annotator agreement. If experts do not agree, tune the rubric before the judge.
  3. Report kappa beside raw agreement, and report true-positive and true-negative rates for pass/fail judges rather than a single accuracy. The 2026 audit distilled its findings into a minimal validation protocol along these lines [1].
  4. Run both orders for every pairwise comparison and treat verdict flips as position sensitivity, not noise. Track preference fairness and repetition stability separately [4].
  5. Rerun and report variance. Use several runs per item and publish the spread next to the score. Treat a gap smaller than your rerun range as unresolved.
  6. Cross the model family. Do not let the same family generate and grade; if you must, add a different-family judge and compare [5][6].
  7. Revalidate on every change to judge model, rubric or data distribution [2].
  8. Route disagreements to humans. Items where judge and expert diverge are your most informative data; feed the resolved labels back into the calibration set.

What to watch next

Three things. First, whether the primary papers behind the secondary claims here, notably the annotator-pool audit and the tool-calling evaluator audit, hold up under independent replication. Second, whether judge validation converges on a standard report (chance-corrected agreement, bias probes, rerun variance, audit trail), as the 2026 audit [1] and the upgrade study [2] both urge, because comparability across papers is currently poor. Third, agent-specific meta-evaluation: labelled trajectory sets with expert disagreement recorded, which would let teams test the claims above on the transcripts they actually grade. Until then, the defensible stance is narrow: use a judge as a triage instrument whose error bars you have measured, and reserve experts for the close calls.
