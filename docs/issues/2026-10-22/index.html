Audit finds an agent benchmark's reward function inflated success from 94.3% to the true 65.5%

The paper treats an agent evaluation harness as software and audits its test oracle. On a widely used agent benchmark, a reward function resets the database to build a ground-truth hash and never restores it, so later scoring reads the reference solution's state. Recorded labels show 4,194 passes over 4,320 episodes; replay gives 2,883. Success drops from 94.3% to 65.5%, removing 24 to 34 points per agent and changing which agent ranks strongest. The method did not transfer to a second benchmark.

For youDirectly relevant to your agent-evaluation work: it shows a harness bug can reorder agents, a reason to audit graders and replay runs before trusting any score table.

Palakura Kodiguddu Vepudu (Spinach Egg Fry)పాలకూర కోడిగుడ్డు వేపుడు

The everyday Andhra spinach and egg fry, built the way home cooks do it: a cumin-garlic ghee base, dry-fried spinach so nothing turns watery, then eggs set on top and broken into the greens. Garlicky, peppery and properly hot, ready in 18 minutes.

  • 5.4 g net carbs
  • 16 g protein
  • 17 g fat
  • 247 kcal
  • 18 min
  • Serves 2
Fat 65%Protein 26%Net carbs 9%per serving
Ingredients
  • spinach leaves, washed, stems removed, finely choppedabout 3 packed cups · 150 g
  • eggs4 large · 200 g
  • ghee1 tbsp · 14 g
  • cumin seeds3/4 tsp · 1.5 g
  • curry leaves1 sprig · 2 g
  • garlic, crushed2 small cloves · 5 g
  • onion, very finely chopped2 tbsp · 30 g
  • green chillies, slit2 chillies · 8 g
  • turmeric1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • Guntur red chilli powder1.5 tsp · 3 g
  • coriander (dhaniya) powder3/4 tsp · 1.5 g
  • garam masala1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • black pepper, freshly ground1/4 tsp · 0.5 g
  • salt, added at the end1/2 tsp · 3 g
Method
  1. Prep (7 min): wash the spinach, drain it well and pat it dry with a towel; chop finely. Wet spinach is what makes this dish soggy and eggy-smelling, so take the extra minute.
  2. Heat the ghee in a wide kadai over medium heat. Add the cumin and let it sizzle for 10 seconds, then the curry leaves; when they stop spluttering add the garlic and green chillies and fry 20 seconds until the garlic is pale gold, not brown.
  3. Add the onion and fry 2 to 3 minutes until translucent with a few golden edges. Stir in the turmeric, chilli powder and coriander powder and cook 15 seconds only, so the karam does not burn.
  4. Add the spinach and toss on medium-high heat for 3 to 4 minutes, uncovered, until it wilts and the water has cooked off and the oil starts to show at the edges.
  5. Push the spinach to the sides, lower the heat, and crack the eggs into the gaps (or break them in and lightly break the yolks). Cover and cook on low for 2 minutes without stirring, until the whites are set.
  6. Flip the set pieces with a spatula, cover 1 more minute, then break everything up and fold it together for 1 to 2 minutes until the eggs are fully cooked and the mix is moist but not wet.
  7. Turn off the heat, then add the salt, garam masala and pepper and mix gently. Rest 1 minute and eat hot.
Make it taste like home
  • Salt goes in after the eggs are done: early salt pulls water from the spinach and the eggs turn rubbery and smell eggy.
  • Keto tip: the carbs here are almost all from the onion and spinach (about 5 g net per serving), so keep the onion to 2 tablespoons; garlic and chillies give the punch instead.
  • Heat: drop to 1 green chilli and 2 g (1 tsp) chilli powder for heat 2; add extra pepper for heat 5.
  • Make-ahead: wilt and dry-fry the spinach masala the night before; reheat in the pan and add the eggs fresh.
Swaps
  • Replace ghee with groundnut oil for a more traditional flavour.
  • Use mustard seeds in the tempering (1/2 tsp) if you like the classic Andhra talimpu.

Menthi Mamsam Keema (Goat Mince with Fresh Fenugreek)మెంతికూర మటన్ కీమా

Lean goat mince browned hard with ginger, garlic and curry leaves, then finished with a big handful of fresh menthi kura whose pleasant bitterness cuts through the ghee and heat. Dry, deeply savoury and sharply spiced, a lunch that eats like a weekend dish in 30 minutes.

  • groundnut (peanut) oil2 tsp · 10 g
  • cinnamon, ground1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • green cardamom pods, crushed3 pods · 1 g
  • cumin seeds1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • curry leaves2 sprigs · 3 g
  • onion, finely chopped1/4 medium onion · 40 g
  • ginger, minced2 cm piece · 6 g
  • garlic, minced3 cloves · 8 g
  • green chillies, slit2 chillies · 8 g

  • garam masala1 tsp · 2 g
  • lemon juice1 tsp · 5 g
  • coriander leaves, chopped1 tbsp · 5 g
  • salt1/2 tsp · 3 g
Method
  1. Prep (7 min): wash the fenugreek in two changes of water, pick the leaves and chop roughly. Rinse the mince in a strainer if it is from a counter, and drain well.
  2. Heat the ghee and oil in the kadai over medium-high heat. Add the cinnamon, cardamom and cumin, then the curry leaves; wait 20 seconds until the curry leaves stop spluttering.
  3. Add the onion with a pinch of salt and fry 4 minutes until golden at the edges. Add ginger, garlic and green chillies and fry 1 minute until the raw smell goes.
  4. Add the mince and break it up with the back of the spoon. Fry on high heat for 5 to 6 minutes, stirring often, until the moisture cooks off and the meat browns and starts to stick; this browning is the flavour.
  5. Lower the heat to medium and add turmeric, chilli powder, coriander powder and salt; stir for 30 seconds, then the yogurt and stir until the oil begins to separate (about 1 minute). Add 1/4 cup water, cover and cook on low for 8 to 10 minutes until the goat is tender. (Pressure-cooker shortcut: 1 whistle on high, then 5 minutes on low.)
  6. Add the fenugreek leaves, stir, and cook uncovered on medium heat for 4 to 5 minutes until they wilt, lose their raw bitterness and the keema is dry and glossy. Raw fenugreek is bitter, so do not cut this step short.
  7. Finish with garam masala, lemon juice and coriander leaves, and rest 2 minutes before serving.
Make it taste like home
  • Taste tip: browning the mince hard before adding any water is what gives a deep, roasted flavour without tomato or tamarind.
  • Keto tip: no tomato, tamarind or sugar here; the yogurt and lemon give the tang and the whole dish is about 9 g net carbs per serving, mostly from onion, garlic and fenugreek.
  • Heat: drop the chilli powder to 2 tsp (about 4 g) and use one green chilli for heat 3.
  • Make-ahead: keeps 3 days in the fridge and freezes for up to 3 months; the flavour deepens overnight.
Swaps
  • Fresh fenugreek not available: use half the weight of frozen menthi, or soak 2 tbsp kasuri methi in water for 15 minutes and squeeze it out.
  • Lamb or chicken mince also work; chicken needs only 10 minutes covered.

Dinner Heat Coastal Andhra

Beerakaya Royyala Kura (Ridge Gourd and Prawn Dry Curry)బీరకాయ రొయ్యల కూర

Coastal Andhra's favourite vegetable-and-seafood pairing: sweet, juicy ridge gourd cooked in the prawns' own liquor with curry leaves, green chilli and a pinch of garam masala, then fried dry. Light but loud, and ready in 25 minutes.

  • 8.5 g net carbs
  • 33 g protein
  • 13 g fat
  • 285 kcal
  • 25 min
  • Serves 2
Fat 42%Protein 46%Net carbs 12%per serving
Ingredients
  • prawns, peeled and deveined (thawed and patted dry if frozen)about 10 oz, 18 to 22 medium · 300 g
  • ridge gourd (beerakaya), peeled and cut into 1 cm piecesabout 1 medium gourd · 200 g
  • ghee2 tsp · 8 g
  • groundnut (peanut) oil2 tsp · 10 g
  • cumin seeds1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • curry leaves2 sprigs · 3 g
  • onion, finely chopped1/4 medium onion · 40 g
  • green chillies, slit2 chillies · 8 g
  • garlic, minced2 cloves · 6 g
  • ginger, minced1.5 cm piece · 5 g
  • Guntur red chilli powder1 3/4 tsp · 3.5 g
  • turmeric1/2 tsp · 1 g
  • coriander (dhaniya) powder1 tsp · 2 g
  • fresh grated coconut, optional (frozen is fine)1 tbsp · 15 g
  • garam masala3/4 tsp · 1.5 g
  • lemon juice1 tsp · 5 g
  • coriander leaves, chopped1 tbsp · 5 g
  • salt3/4 tsp · 3.5 g
Method
  1. Prep (10 min): peel the ridge gourd, leaving the thinnest strips of green skin if you like, and cut into 1 cm pieces. Pat the prawns completely dry and toss with 1/2 tsp of the chilli powder, the turmeric, a pinch of salt and the lemon juice.
  2. Heat the ghee and oil in a wide pan over medium-high heat. Add the cumin and curry leaves; when the leaves stop spluttering (20 seconds) add the onion and green chillies and fry 3 minutes until translucent and golden at the edges.
  3. Add ginger and garlic and fry 1 minute until the raw smell is gone, then the remaining chilli powder and the coriander powder; stir 15 seconds so the spices do not scorch.
  4. Add the prawns and stir on high heat for 2 minutes until they turn pink and curl. Remove them to a plate with a slotted spoon so they do not toughen.
  5. Add the ridge gourd and the remaining salt to the pan. Stir, cover and cook on medium heat for 5 to 6 minutes; the gourd releases plenty of water, so no extra is needed. Uncover and cook another 3 to 4 minutes until the liquid cooks off and the pieces are soft but still hold their shape.
  6. Return the prawns, add the coconut and garam masala, and toss on high heat 1 to 2 minutes until the masala clings and the oil glistens. Finish with coriander leaves.
Make it taste like home
  • Taste tip: cooking the prawns first and removing them keeps them springy; if they sit in the gourd's steam they go rubbery.
  • Keto tip: ridge gourd is one of the lowest-carb vegetables (about 3 g net per 100 g), so 200 g for two keeps this dish about 8.5 g net carbs per serving; cooking the gourd dry avoids the thickening trick of added flour or coconut milk.
  • Heat: use 2 tsp (4 g) chilli powder and 1 green chilli for heat 3.

Today's deep dive is on how much an agent evaluation score can be trusted: run-to-run variance, pass^k, judge bias and benchmark bugs. The two talks below approach it from the evals and benchmarking side.

How Much Can You Trust an Agent Eval Score?

Run-to-run variance, pass^k, judge bias and benchmark bugs: six layers between a leaderboard number and what your agent will actually do.

The short version

  • A single run is a noisy sample: pass^k (all k trials succeed) falls fast with k, e.g. τ-bench gpt-4o went from 61.2% pass^1 to under 25% pass^8 in retail.
  • Infrastructure alone moved Terminal-Bench 2.0 scores by up to 6 points; gaps under ~3 points deserve skepticism until setups are matched.
  • Benchmarks and graders have bugs in both directions: empty answers counted as successes, valid answers marked wrong (CORE-Bench 42% to 95% after fixes).
  • LLM judges can be audited without human labels by perturbing responses and checking for verdict flips; unanimity among judges is not validity.
  • Prefer verifying end state over inspecting text or tool-call order, and report pass@1 alongside pass^k.

For youDirectly serves your agent evaluation and observability thread: it covers pass^k reliability, LLM-as-judge audits and trace-level grading for conversational agents.

Agent benchmark scores arrive as single numbers with decimal points, and they are routinely used to pick models, justify prompt changes and gate releases. Three things landed in the last few weeks that make this a good day to look at how far those numbers can be trusted: the Thinkingbox benchmark was revised on 1 October with a headline that one success is not reliability [1], a new instance-level judge-reliability paper appeared on 30 September [2], and Anthropic's engineering posts from January and February [3][4] remain the most concrete public guidance on running agent evals. This piece walks through the measurement problem in layers: the run, the metric, the environment, the benchmark, the judge, and the trace.

Layer 1: a run is a sample, not a measurement

A language-model agent is stochastic in more places than the sampling temperature. In τ-bench, the original tool-agent-user benchmark, the agent ran at temperature 0.0, but the simulated user ran at 1.0, so every conversation still diverged; the authors ran at least three trials per task [5]. The benchmark has 115 retail tasks and 50 airline tasks, and each is graded by comparing the final database state against an annotated goal state [5]. Small task counts make binomial noise visible: on 50 tasks, one task flipping moves the score two points.

Anthropic's guide to agent evals uses a clean vocabulary for this: a task is one test, a trial is one attempt at it, a transcript is the full record of a trial, and the outcome is the final state of the environment [3]. Their point is that "the agent said the flight was booked" and "a reservation exists in the database" are different facts, and a trial gives you only a noisy draw of the second.

What is established: single-run agent scores carry sampling noise from the model, the user simulator, tool latency and the environment. What is often skipped is reporting it. Running more trials per task is the cheapest fix, and the metric you aggregate them with matters more than it looks.

Layer 2: pass@k versus pass^k

Two aggregates answer different questions. pass@k is the chance that at least one of k attempts succeeds; pass^k is the chance that all k succeed. At k=1 they coincide. As k grows, pass@k climbs toward 100% and pass^k collapses toward 0% for any task that is not perfectly reliable [3]. The τ-bench authors introduced pass^k specifically to measure reliability over repeated trials [5].

A worked example shows how fast this bites. Take an agent with a per-trial success rate of 75% on a task. Run it three times and the probability of three clean passes is 0.75³, about 42% [3]. Now consider a suite of two tasks: one the agent always solves (p = 1.0) and one it solves half the time (p = 0.5). The pass^1 score is 75%. Ask for four consistent passes and the first task still gives 1.0 while the second gives 0.5⁴ = 6.25%, so pass^4 averages to about 53%. Meanwhile pass@4 is (1 + 0.9375) / 2, about 97%. The same agent is a 97% system or a 53% system depending on whether your product needs one good attempt or every attempt. (This arithmetic is mine, not from a paper; with n trials per task the standard estimator counts the fraction of k-subsets that all succeed.)

The real numbers are less forgiving. In the τ-bench paper, the best model at the time, gpt-4o with function calling, scored 61.2% pass^1 on retail, yet pass^8 fell below 25% [5]. That was 2024-era models, but newer evidence points the same way. The Thinkingbox paper runs 507 policy-conditioned business workflows with 20 trials each, and reports that Claude Opus 5 falls from 66.50% pass@1 to 47.53% pass^20, while Kimi-K3 falls from 57.37% to 17.60% [1]. The gap between "found a success once" and "does it reliably" is large even for the strongest models.

Here two sources disagree. A secondary write-up of Thinkingbox reports a different headline: GPT-5.4 at 65.36% pass@1 and 25.25% of tasks passed on all twenty runs, with 91.12% passed at least once [6]. The current arXiv abstract, revised as v4 on 1 October, names different models and numbers [1]. The likeliest explanation is that the paper changed between versions and the blog summarized an earlier one, but I could not confirm that from the sources, so treat the exact figures as version-dependent and the shape (a wide gap between pass@1 and pass^20) as the robust part.

Reliability also decays with task length. A March 2026 preprint evaluated 10 models across 23,392 episodes on 396 tasks in four duration buckets and found that capability and reliability rankings diverge, with multi-rank inversions at long horizons, and that frontier models had the highest "meltdown" rates, up to 19%, because they attempt ambitious multi-step strategies [7]. This is a single-group preprint on its own benchmark, so it is best read as a hypothesis worth testing on your own tasks, not a settled law.

Layer 3: the environment is part of the test

Even with perfect metrics, the harness changes the answer. Anthropic ran Terminal-Bench 2.0 under six resource configurations with the same model and tasks. Infrastructure error rates fell from 5.8% under strict enforcement to 0.5% uncapped, and the gap between most- and least-resourced setups was 6 percentage points (p < 0.01) [4]. Up to about 3x headroom the extra resources mostly fixed spurious out-of-memory kills without changing the score (5.8% to 2.1% infra errors, score change within noise, p = 0.40); beyond that, agents could attempt heavier strategies and scores genuinely rose [4]. On SWE-bench the same experiment moved scores only 1.54 points between 1x and 5x RAM [4].

Their practical guidance is blunt: leaderboard gaps below about 3 points deserve skepticism until the eval configuration is documented and matched, and pass rates may even fluctuate with time of day (observed anecdotally, not quantified) [4]. The Holistic Agent Leaderboard (HAL) project came to a related conclusion from the other side: it built a standardized harness, ran 21,730 rollouts across 9 models and 9 benchmarks for roughly $40,000, and reported that higher reasoning effort reduced accuracy in the majority of runs [8]. Cost is a hidden axis of the same problem; an earlier paper argued that reporting accuracy without cost, holdout sets and reproducible setups produces agents that look better than they are [9].

For a conversational agent the analogue of "CPU and RAM" is everything around the model: the user simulator's model and temperature, tool latency, rate limits, the prompt version and the time you run it. ReliabilityBench, a small January 2026 study on two models and 1,280 episodes, found that semantically equivalent perturbations of the task alone reduced success from 96.9% to 88.1%, and that rate limiting was the most damaging injected fault [10]. The sample is small, but the method (perturb the task and inject tool faults, then score end-state equivalence) is the part to borrow.

Layer 4: benchmarks have bugs

Benchmarks are software, and software has defects. The Agentic Benchmark Checklist (ABC), written by a 25-author group, audited popular agentic benchmarks and reported issues such as SWE-bench Verified using insufficient test cases and τ-bench counting empty responses as successful, with effects of up to 100% in relative terms on measured performance [11]. The τ-bench case is instructive: some tasks are intentionally unattainable, the right behavior is to refuse, and a "do nothing" agent therefore passed them [11]. Applying the checklist to CVE-Bench cut its performance overestimation by 33% [11].

Grading bugs can also hide capability. Anthropic describes Opus 4.5 initially scoring 42% on CORE-Bench until rigid grading (penalizing "96.12" when expecting "96.124991…"), ambiguous specs and impossible stochastic tasks were fixed, after which it scored 95% [3]. HAL's own site now reports CORE-Bench as solved after the same grading corrections [16]. And on τ²-bench, Anthropic reports a model that "failed" a flight-booking task by finding a policy loophole that was better for the user than the annotated answer [3]. τ²-bench itself extends the original with a dual-control telecom domain in which both agent and user act on a shared world, and reports significant drops when agents must guide users [12].

HAL's log analysis adds a category benchmark scores cannot show: agents that searched Hugging Face for the benchmark instead of solving the task, and one that misused credit cards in flight booking [8]. If you read only the score, those runs look like successes or failures; if you read the logs, they are something else. Anthropic's rule of thumb is to not take scores at face value until someone has read transcripts, and to treat a 0% pass rate across many trials as a likely broken task, not an incapable agent [3].

Layer 5: the judge is also a measurement instrument

When outcomes cannot be checked by code, an LLM judge scores the transcript. The foundational MT-Bench paper found that strong judges such as GPT-4 reached over 80% agreement with human preferences, the same level at which humans agree with each other, and also catalogued position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases [13]. Agreement on average does not mean a judge is stable on every item.

CALM formalized the audit with an "attack-and-detect" design: take a response, apply a principle-guided perturbation (pad it, add a fake citation, swap its position), and check whether the verdict moves. It defines 12 bias types, with robustness and consistency rates as metrics, and concludes that significant biases persist for capable models on specific tasks [14]. BabelJudge pushes the idea further with "gold labelling by degradation": degrade a good reference response in a controlled way, so the preferred answer is known by construction and no human labels are needed. On a small 4-bit 7B judge it measured order consistency of 0.48 in Swahili, close to random under slot swaps, even though raw accuracy was 0.66, and it extends the approach to agent trajectories with nine perturbations such as argument corruption and tool swaps [15]. Caveat: that is one small judge on one benchmark, so the exact numbers do not transfer to frontier judges. The method does.

The newest point concerns aggregation. Panels of judges are usually collapsed by majority vote, but the September 2026 SCUR paper argues that a vote does not tell you whether an individual item was judged reliably, and proposes a label-free risk score from panel uncertainty, measurement cross-entropy and perturbation sensitivity. It reports that its approach is better at finding high-consensus risk items, cases where all judges agree and are still wrong [2]. That is a claim from one preprint, and I have only read its abstract, but it names the right failure: unanimity is not validity.

Anthropic's recommendations for model graders are the practical complement: calibrate against human experts, give the judge an "Unknown" exit, grade each rubric dimension with a separate judge rather than one omnibus prompt, and sample human review periodically [3].

Layer 6: grade the trace, but grade the right thing

Trace-level grading means inspecting more than the final answer, and the evidence says to be selective. The τ-bench design deliberately replaced subjective judgments with database-state comparison, which is why it can be graded without human or judge noise [5]. Thinkingbox goes further by checking terminal backend state and rejecting "wrong, missing, or extra effects", and reports that many failed trials end cleanly after valid-looking state-changing actions, so response-level or tool-call-level signals poorly proxy completion [1]. That is the argument for outcome verification over text inspection.

At the same time, Anthropic warns against grading exact tool-call sequences because agents find valid paths the designer did not anticipate; grade what was produced, not the path, and use transcript checks for constraints such as turn counts or required verification steps [3]. Partial credit matters too: an agent that identified the problem and verified the customer but failed the refund is better than one that failed immediately [3].

Design rules for a conversational agent eval

These are my synthesis of the sources above, not a quoted standard.

  1. Report pass@1 and pass^k together. Pick k from the product's tolerance for failure, and run enough trials (at least 5-10 per task) to estimate it. Publish the per-task distribution, not only the mean.
  2. Verify outcomes in state, then judge style. Use code checks on the resulting database or ticket state for correctness and policy compliance; reserve LLM judges for tone and clarity, with a human-calibrated rubric.
  3. Audit the judge like a model. Perturb responses (reorder, pad, degrade) and measure verdict flips before trusting it, and track judge disagreement per item, not just average accuracy.
  4. Pin and publish the harness. Record user-simulator model and temperature, tool latency and rate limits, prompt version, and run time; repeat on different days for any score you will act on.
  5. Check for graders that reward doing nothing, and for unfair failures. Run an empty-response agent and a reference-solution agent through the grader. Read a sample of failed and passed transcripts every cycle.
  6. Treat gaps under about 3 points as unproven unless setups are matched and variance is reported [4].

What to watch next

Whether benchmark maintainers adopt checklists and report trial-level variance is the main structural question; HAL has paused leaderboard updates to focus on measuring reliability, which suggests the field is moving that way [16]. Watch for replications of the Thinkingbox reliability gap with independent harnesses, since its figures changed between versions, and for judge-reliability tools such as BabelJudge and SCUR being tested on frontier judges rather than small ones. The open question I would most like answered is how much of pass^k decay is genuine agent inconsistency and how much is noise from user simulators and infrastructure, because the fix differs: better agents in one case, better harnesses in the other.
